## Key points

- A **development approach** is how the product is created and evolved: **predictive**, **adaptive** or **hybrid**, best seen as a spectrum. It is not the same as a project **phase**.
- **Predictive** fits when scope can be stabilized early, change is costly, safety or regulation demand up-front planning, or a fixed date must be met.
- **Adaptive** fits high uncertainty and volatility, innovation, value from frequent feedback, and early delivery. Iterations are typically 1 to 4 weeks.
- **Hybrid** combines them; the Eighth Edition says most projects can strongly benefit from it. Four patterns: adaptive then predictive rollout; both side by side; mostly predictive with an adaptive part; mostly adaptive with a predictive part.
- Choose by **deliverables**, **project** and **organization** factors. Assess complexity with the **Stacey matrix** or **Cynefin**.
- **Tailoring** has four steps: select the approach, tailor for the organization, tailor for the project, implement ongoing improvement. It never ends.

## Life cycles and phases

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.1

A **project life cycle** is the series of activities and phases a project passes through from start to closure. A **phase** is a collection of logically related activities that ends with one or more deliverables or outcomes. Phases can be **sequential**, **transitional** or **overlapping**.

**Phase gates.** A gate after a phase (sometimes also a **pre-phase gate** at its start) reviews whether the exit criteria or desired outcomes have been achieved. The possible decisions:

| Decision |
|---|
| Continue to the next phase |
| Continue with modification |
| End the project or phase |
| Remain in the phase |
| Repeat the phase or part of it |
| Park the project temporarily for an urgent business initiative |

Organizations often adopt a few **standard life cycles**; project managers may need to advise on the most appropriate one, or it may be prescribed.

**Change over time.** In many projects, stakeholders' ability to influence scope **cheaply** is highest at the start and falls over time, while **risk and uncertainty fall** and the **cost of changes rises** as the project progresses.

## The three development approaches

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.1; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.2; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.3

| | Predictive | Adaptive | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Also called** | Waterfall, plan-driven, traditional | Change-driven, agile | Combined |
| **Fits when** | Scope can be **stabilized early**; large investment or **regulatory oversight**; the cost of iterating far exceeds its value (the build phase of construction) | Requirements and solution are **uncertain and volatile**; design and new product development; digital projects with evolving scope | Uncertainty around requirements (the design of a custom home, then one build); modular deliverables; deliverables built by different teams |
| **Planning** | Much of it up front; robust change control; planned reevaluation between phases | A clear **vision** first; requirements progressively elaborated by feedback; the whole team plans at several levels | Each part planned the way that suits it |
| **Delivery** | Usually one release; can be **incremental** when scope is known but phased delivery helps | **Iterative** (repeated cycles, typically 1 to 4 weeks, with a demonstration) and **incremental** (small usable segments); or **flow-based** | Per part |

**Incremental predictive delivery**: the scope is planned up front, but the product is delivered in parts; stakeholders get value earlier, while overall scope and timeline stay largely fixed.

**Agile methods** differ in **breadth** (how much of the life cycle they cover) and **depth** (how prescriptive they are): Scrum, XP, Kanban, Lean, DSDM, Crystal, FDD, Agile UP; scaled methods such as SAFe, LeSS and Scrum of Scrums. A **method** is a systematic way of doing something; a **tool kit** offers options to choose from: **PMI Disciplined Agile is a tool kit**.

**Hybrid.** "In practice, most projects can strongly benefit from a hybrid approach": flexibility where conditions change, control over predictable aspects. Four common patterns:

1. **Adaptive development followed by a predictive rollout** (a new high-tech product, then rollout and training for thousands of users);
2. **Adaptive and predictive used simultaneously** throughout;
3. **A largely predictive approach with an adaptive component**;
4. **A largely adaptive approach with a predictive component**.

Examples: a smart building (predictive construction, adaptive IT); software (adaptive) installed in a new data center (predictive).

## Assessing needs, complexity and magnitude

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.10; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.1; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.2

The first enabler of Process task 1 is to **assess project needs, complexity and magnitude**.

**Complexity and uncertainty** (Agile Practice Guide):

- Defined, repeatable work with little uncertainty suits "**plan the work, work the plan**". Intangible, novel **knowledge work** (software, filmmaking) rarely progresses that predictably.
- Many projects operate in **complex adaptive systems**: parts interact in nonlinear ways, cause and effect are hard to trace, and teams benefit from learning, experimentation and incremental progress.

| Model | How it assesses | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Stacey matrix** | Two dimensions: agreement on **what** is needed (requirements uncertainty) and certainty on **how** to deliver (technical uncertainty) | Both certain (lower left): predictive works well. As either becomes uncertain: complicated, complex or chaotic regions, where adaptive or exploratory life cycles help |
| **Cynefin Framework** | Five domains: **simple** (obvious cause and effect), **complicated** (expert analysis), **complex** (outcomes emerge), **chaotic** (act to stabilize), **disorder** (unclear) | Complex: **probe, sense, respond**, as in agile. Complicated: analysis, possibly a hybrid of planning phases and iterative execution |

**Magnitude.** Scale changes how much structure is needed: the rigor and reporting for a critical **nuclear reactor** project are far greater than for a new office building, and a 10-person team needs far less coordination than a team of 200.

## Recommending a development approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.3; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.3.1; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.3.2; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.3.3; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.4.1

The second enabler is to **recommend a development approach**. The Eighth Edition groups the factors in three categories; several approaches may be used in one project for different deliverables.

| Category | Points toward **adaptive** | Points toward **predictive** |
|---|---|---|
| **Deliverables** | High **innovation**; unstable scope; **risk and uncertainty** to manage; much value from frequent **feedback**; deliverable can be delivered in parts | Well-known **requirements**; changes difficult or costly; rigorous **safety** requirements; significant **regulation** |
| **Project** | Value from delivering something early; **financing uncertainty**; larger, complex projects with evolving requirements; team with strong collaboration skills; strong stakeholder involvement | A **fixed end date** to plan toward |
| **Organization** | Network-oriented structure; a culture that embraces uncertainty and self-managed teams; small teams (some frameworks: **three to nine** members), sometimes colocated | Functional, hierarchical structure; a culture of managing and directing against baselines |

Interdependencies with other projects or departments are also a factor; so is **organizational capability**: policies, reporting and attitudes must support the chosen approach.

**The suitability filter** is a decision-making tool, not a rigid procedure: it evaluates culture, team and project factors to help decide the **initial** approach, together with other data.

> **On the exam.** Recommend the approach the factors point to, and say why. A project with a fixed regulatory submission at the end and an uncertain product design inside it is a typical **hybrid**. "Agile everywhere" or "predictive everywhere" is rarely the best answer.

## Delivery cadence and the execution strategy

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.4; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.3; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.3

The third enabler is to **recommend a project execution strategy**: how the work will be delivered.

| Delivery cadence | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Single delivery** | All outcomes at the end | A process reengineering project rolled out near the end |
| **Multiple deliveries** | Components delivered at different times, in sequence or in parallel | A new drug: preclinical submissions, then each clinical trial phase; a building security upgrade delivered as separate items |
| **Periodic deliveries** | Like multiple, but on a **regular, fixed schedule** | Internal software deliveries every 2 weeks, released to market periodically |
| **Continuous delivery** | Tested, production-ready increments delivered to production on an ongoing basis | Frequent automated deployment that responds quickly to feedback |

**Combining approaches.** A single approach for the whole project is not necessary. A typical execution strategy uses agile development where there is uncertainty and risk, then a predictive rollout that is defined and repeatable, perhaps by a different team. Constraints can shape the strategy: in the Agile Practice Guide's case, a drug company's regulatory approval had to stay a separate final phase, so the team shortened it with quick-start guides and testing protocols instead of iterating it.

## Tailoring

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 3.1; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.2; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.3.2; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.3.3; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.4; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.4.3; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.4.4; PMBOK 8, Guide 3.6

**Tailoring** is the deliberate adaptation of the approach, governance and processes to the project's environment and objectives, driven by the principles. It balances competing demands: speed, cost, value, quality, compliance and sustainability, stakeholder expectations, adapting to change. Organizational policy or a contract may limit it.

**Why tailor?** There is no single approach for all projects. **Too few processes** make project management ineffective; **too many** are costly and wasteful. Benefits: more **commitment** from the team that helped tailor; a stronger customer focus; more efficient use of resources.

**What to tailor:** the life cycle and development approach; **processes** (add, modify, remove, blend, align); and **engagement** (people's skills, how much decision-making is empowered to the team, and integrating contributors from several organizations into one team).

**The four steps:**

1. **Select the initial development approach** (for example with a suitability filter);
2. **Tailor for the organization**: organizations provide methodologies as starting points, aligned with policy and with related projects;
3. **Tailor for the project**: by the product (standards, type, market, technology, timeframe), the team (experience, access to the customer, diversity) and the culture (buy-in, trust, empowerment, organizational culture);
4. **Implement ongoing improvement**: tailoring is **not a one-time exercise**; review points, phase gates and retrospectives are chances to inspect and adapt.

**Diagnostics.** Retrospectives and lessons learned show whether the approach works; teams without retrospectives can use issues, threats, quality metrics and stakeholder feedback.

## Exam tips

- Approach is a spectrum; most real projects are hybrid.
- Uncertainty, innovation, feedback value: adaptive. Stable requirements, costly change, safety, regulation, fixed date: predictive.
- Use the Stacey matrix or Cynefin to judge complexity; probe, sense, respond in the complex domain.
- Choose a delivery cadence: single, multiple, periodic or continuous.
- Tailor, then keep tailoring: too little process fails, too much wastes.
