## Key points

- The **project vision** sums up the project's purpose clearly and briefly: a realistic, attractive picture of the future that the outcomes will bring. It motivates, and it guides everyday decisions.
- A vision is **built together** with team members and key stakeholders, not handed down. It answers four questions: the purpose, what successful work looks like, how the future will be better, and how the team will know it is drifting.
- A good vision is **clear, concise and actionable**: a short phrase, the best achievable outcome, one picture in everyone's mind, passion for the result.
- **Promote** it by referring to it: in decisions, in problem-solving, in planning, and wherever the team can see it.
- **Keep it current**: review it regularly, link the roadmap and the backlog to it, and rework it with stakeholders when the situation changes.
- When people **misunderstand** the vision, treat it as a problem to solve: define it, find the root cause, then act. Do not just repeat the vision louder.

## What a project vision is

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership; PMBOK 8, Glossary (vision statement)

The Eighth Edition lists **establishing and maintaining vision** first among the activities of leadership. Every project has a purpose, and people commit their time and energy in the right direction only when they understand it.

| | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Project vision** | A clear, brief summary of the project's purpose: a realistic, attractive view of the future outcomes |
| **Why it matters** | It is a strong motivator, gives the work meaning, keeps people pulling in the same direction, and guides local decisions when the team is deep in daily detail |
| **Vision statement** (glossary) | A short, high-level description of what is expected of a **product**: target market, users, major benefits, and what sets it apart |

**The four questions** a vision should answer:

1. What is the project's purpose?
2. What defines successful project work?
3. How will the future be better when the outcomes are delivered?
4. How will the project team know that it is drifting from the vision?

**A good vision** is clear, concise and actionable. It:

- sums up the project in a powerful phrase or short description,
- describes the best achievable outcome,
- creates a common, cohesive picture in the team members' minds, and
- inspires passion for the outcome.

> **On the exam.** A vision is not a list of requirements, a schedule or a scope statement. If an answer option describes detailed deliverables, it is not the vision. The fourth question matters on PMP: a vision that says how drift will be noticed gives the team a way to check itself.

## Building a shared vision with key stakeholders

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Project charter; Agile Practice Guide, 5.1; Agile Practice Guide, 5.1.1; Agile Practice Guide, 3.5.1.2; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Facilitation

The first enabler of the outline's task is to **help ensure a shared vision with key stakeholders**. "Shared" is the point: the Eighth Edition says the vision should be **developed collaboratively** by project team members and key stakeholders.

**Where the vision is set down**

| Setting | Where the vision lives | Who leads |
|---|---|---|
| Any project | The **project charter** tells the team why the project matters, where it is headed and what its objective is | Sponsor or initiator issues it |
| Agile project | An **agile project charter**, lightweight, answering: Why are we doing this (the vision and objectives)? Who benefits and how? What does "done" mean for the project (release criteria)? How will we work together? | A supportive leader may facilitate it with the team |
| Product | The **product vision** and success metrics | Product manager or product owner |

The Agile Practice Guide adds that a project charter alone may not be enough for the team: **at a minimum**, an agile team should establish the project vision or purpose and a clear set of working agreements.

**Running the vision session.** Building a shared vision is a facilitated group event. A facilitator helps ensure that everyone takes part, that participants reach a mutual understanding, that all contributions are considered, that the result has full buy-in under the agreed decision process, and that what is agreed is followed up afterward.

> **On the exam.** When stakeholders hold different pictures of the project's purpose early on, the strongest answer brings them together to agree it (a workshop, a chartering session), rather than the project manager writing a vision alone and sending it out, or starting the work and hoping the views converge.

## Promoting the vision

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.1; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3; Agile Practice Guide, 5.1.2; Agile Practice Guide, Table 5-2

The second enabler is to **promote the shared vision**. Agreeing a vision once is not enough; it has to be used.

- **Communicate it throughout the project.** The Eighth Edition names "vision and objectives" first among the common aspects of team development. They are communicated throughout the project, including by **referring to the intended outcomes when the team makes decisions and solves problems**.
- **Make it a shared understanding.** High-performing teams hold the purpose of the project and its benefits in common. This shared understanding is close to project alignment: the project's goals and activities in line with the organization's mission and strategy.
- **Purpose comes first.** A supportive leader works in a set order: first **purpose** (define the "why" with the team so it can coalesce around the goal), then **people** (an environment where everyone can succeed), then **process** (look for results, not a "perfect" process).
- **Keep it in sight.** Agile teams post their charter where the team can see it, and a team charter carries the team's values alongside the vision.

> **On the exam.** "Promoting" the vision on PMP means *using* it: pointing back to it when a team is choosing between options, when a stakeholder asks for a feature that does not serve it, or when a new member joins. A poster with no use in decisions does not count.

## Keeping the vision current

@source Agile Practice Guide, Table 5-2; Agile Practice Guide, Table 5-3; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Agile release planning

The third enabler is to **keep the vision current**. A vision drifts out of date when the market, the organization or the project's understanding of the need changes.

| Practice | Source |
|---|---|
| Review the vision **regularly**; a project brief or team charter states the vision, the mission and simple success tests | Agile Practice Guide, pain point "unclear purpose or mission" |
| When requirements are unclear or keep changing, **cocreate** a product vision and a lightweight roadmap, for example with story mapping | Agile Practice Guide, pain point "unclear or changing requirements" |
| When several teams work on one product and the vision has **fragmented**: run a joint vision workshop with leaders, product owners and team representatives; write **one** vision statement and one lightweight roadmap that all teams use; keep a shared product goal visible in release planning; **confirm every backlog item links back to the vision** | Agile Practice Guide, scaling pain point "fragmented product vision" |
| The vision **drives** the roadmap, the roadmap drives release plans, release plans set the iterations | PMBOK 8, agile release planning (Figure 5-1) |

Because the vision sits at the top of that chain, a change to it ripples down. When the vision changes, the roadmap and the backlog are reviewed against it; work that no longer serves it is questioned.

> **On the exam.** "Keep current" does not mean "keep unchanged". If the business situation has changed and the vision no longer fits, the right move is to rework it **with** the sponsor, product owner and key stakeholders, then realign the plan or backlog, not to defend the old wording, nor to change it quietly on your own.

## When the vision is misunderstood: finding the root cause

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Problem-solving; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Root cause analysis; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Brainstorming techniques; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.1

The fourth enabler is to **break down situations to identify the root cause of a misunderstanding of the vision**. A misunderstanding is a problem like any other, and the Eighth Edition gives a structured way to solve it:

1. **Define** the problem,
2. **identify the root cause**,
3. generate possible solutions,
4. choose the best one,
5. implement it, and
6. **verify** that it worked.

**Root cause analysis** looks for the basic reason behind a problem; when all root causes are removed, the problem does not recur. **Five whys** keeps asking "why" to get past the symptoms. The Agile Practice Guide gives the same job to a supportive leader: support team members by **identifying the root causes of recurrent problems**.

**Breaking the situation down.** The four vision questions make a useful checklist for finding where the understanding splits. The table is this course's own way of applying them, not a list from the books.

| What you notice | Question the parties answer differently | A likely root cause |
|---|---|---|
| Two groups argue over which features matter | What defines successful project work? | Success was never defined in measurable terms |
| A stakeholder calls the project "pointless" | What is the purpose? How will the future be better? | The stakeholder was not part of building the vision, or the benefit to them was never explained |
| The team builds things nobody asked for | How will we know we are drifting? | No link between the backlog and the vision |
| A new team member works in the wrong direction | All four | The vision is not part of how new members are brought in |

> **On the exam.** A question that says people "misunderstand" or "have different interpretations of" the vision is asking you to **find out why first**, by talking with those involved, before choosing a fix. Repeating the vision statement, escalating, or replacing people are not first steps.

## Predictive and agile, side by side

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.1; Agile Practice Guide, 5.1.1; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3

This comparison is this course's summary of the sections above.

| | Predictive | Agile and hybrid |
|---|---|---|
| Where the vision is first written | Project charter, issued by the sponsor | Agile project charter and product vision, built with the team; facilitated by a supportive leader |
| Who keeps it | Project manager with the sponsor | Product owner for the product vision; the whole team for its own purpose |
| How it is kept alive | Referred to in decisions, problem-solving and reviews | Referred to in planning, backlog refinement and reviews; every backlog item traces to it |
| When it changes | Agreed again with the sponsor, who issued the charter | Reworked with stakeholders, then the roadmap and backlog follow |

## Exam tips

- Vision = purpose, success, a better future, and a way to notice drift. Short, inspiring, shared.
- **Build** it with key stakeholders; **promote** it by using it in decisions; **keep** it current by reviewing it and linking work to it; **diagnose** a misunderstanding before fixing it.
- One product, many teams: one vision statement, one roadmap, every backlog item linked back.
- A supportive leader starts with **purpose**, then people, then process.
