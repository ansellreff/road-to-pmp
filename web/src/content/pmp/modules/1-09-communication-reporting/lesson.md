## Key points

- The **communications management plan** says how, when and by whom information is administered and disseminated: requirements, content and format, methods, frequency, who sends, who receives, who authorizes confidential releases, and **escalation processes**. It is a **dynamic** document.
- **Communication requirements analysis** finds stakeholders' information needs, including the number of **communication channels**: **N × (N − 1) / 2**.
- **Methods**: **interactive** (real-time, multidirectional), **push** (sent to recipients; does not ensure understanding), **pull** (recipients access it, for large information sets or audiences).
- **Models**: the basic model (encode, transmit, decode) ensures delivery; the **interactive model** adds **acknowledge** and **feedback**, to ensure understanding. Noise is anything that gets in the way.
- **Reports** are adapted to each audience's level, format and detail: status reports, work performance reports, dashboards, presentations. Agile teams report forecasts to sponsors as **ranges**, never as single-point commitments.
- Governance needs target **metrics**, **signaling mechanisms** (mostly **leading indicators**) and **feedback mechanisms**. Leading indicators warn early; **lagging** indicators report what has happened.

## Defining a communication strategy

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.3; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 2-35; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication requirements analysis; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication technology; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Communications management plan

People task 8's first enabler is to **define a communication strategy**. In the Eighth Edition this is the process **Plan Communications Management**: planning how to communicate with stakeholders inside and outside the team. It overlaps with identifying, analyzing and engaging stakeholders (modules 1.5 and 1.6) and is closely aligned with the stakeholder engagement plan.

**Communication requirements analysis** determines stakeholders' information needs, through interviews, workshops and lessons from previous projects, by combining the **type and format** of information with its **value**. It draws on:

- the stakeholder register and the stakeholder engagement plan;
- the **number of potential communication channels** (one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many);
- organizational charts, responsibilities, relationships and interdependencies;
- the development approach; the disciplines and departments involved; how many people at which locations;
- internal and external information needs (for example the media, the public, contractors); **legal requirements**.

**Choosing communication technology** depends on: the **urgency** of the information; the **availability and reliability** of the technology for all stakeholders; **ease of use** (with training if needed); the **project environment** (face-to-face or virtual, time zones, languages, culture); and the **sensitivity and confidentiality** of the information, including social media policies.

**The communications management plan** describes how, when and by whom information will be administered and disseminated. It contains, among other things:

| Item | Example |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder communication requirements | The sponsor needs a monthly one-page summary |
| Information: language, format, content, level of detail | Executive summary in English; full data for the team |
| **Escalation processes** | When and to whom issues are raised |
| Reason for distributing it | Decision, action or awareness |
| Timeframe and frequency; acknowledgment expected | Weekly, with a reply within two days |
| Who communicates; **who authorizes release of confidential information** | The project manager; the legal officer |
| Who receives it, with their needs and expectations | The steering committee |
| Methods and technologies | Memos, emails, press releases, social media |

The plan is **dynamic**: updated as scope, stakeholder requirements, team structure or the environment change. It helps keep everyone informed and the project **transparent**.

## Communication channels

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication requirements analysis; The Project Management Answer Book, Chapter 11, Q18

The number of potential one-to-one channels in a group of N people is:

**N × (N − 1) / 2**

| People | Calculation | Channels |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 4 × 3 / 2 = 6 | 6 |
| 10 | 10 × 9 / 2 = 45 | 45 |
| 12 | 12 × 11 / 2 = 66 | 66 |

Adding two people to a team of ten adds 66 − 45 = 21 channels. Channels grow much faster than headcount, which is why larger projects need more deliberate communication planning. The formula also shows how many people could leak **confidential** information.

> **On the exam.** Read whether the question asks for the **total** number of channels or the number **added**. If the project manager is included in the count, N includes them.

## Communication methods and models

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication methods; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication models; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication competence

**Communication methods:**

| Method | What it is | Examples | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interactive** | Two or more parties exchanging information in real time, in several directions | Meetings, phone calls, instant messaging, videoconferencing | Questions and feedback can happen at once |
| **Push** | Sent directly to specific recipients | Letters, memos, reports, emails, voicemails, blogs, press releases | Ensures it is **distributed**, not that it **reaches or is understood** |
| **Pull** | Recipients access content at their own discretion, subject to security | Web portals, intranet sites, eLearning, lessons learned databases, knowledge repositories | For **large, complex information sets** or **large audiences** |

The plan also suits the **form** of communication: interpersonal (between individuals, often face-to-face), small group (three to six people), public (a speaker to an audience) and mass (minimal connection to a large, sometimes anonymous audience).

**Communication models:**

| Model | Steps | Concerned with |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic sender/receiver** | **Encode** the message; **transmit** it through a channel, where **noise** may cause loss; **decode** it | That the message is **delivered** |
| **Interactive** | Adds **acknowledge** (receipt only, not agreement or understanding) and **feedback/response** | That the message is **understood** |

The **sender** is responsible for making the message clear and complete and for **confirming it is correctly interpreted**. The **receiver** is responsible for receiving it in full, interpreting it correctly and acknowledging or responding. **Noise** includes distraction, differing perceptions, and lack of knowledge or interest; in the fuller model, both parties' emotional state, knowledge, background, personality, culture and biases shape how messages are sent and received. **Cross-cultural** differences (working methods, age, nationality, discipline) make understanding harder.

**Communication competence** combines tailored skills: clarity of purpose in key messages, effective relationships and information sharing, and leadership behaviors.

## The feedback loop

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication models; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Feedback; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Active listening; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.7; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 2-39

The enabler **establish a feedback loop** works at two levels.

**In every message.** In the interactive model, communication succeeds when the sender sees that the **feedback matches the original message**. Between people, feedback comes through **active listening**: acknowledging, clarifying, confirming and understanding. **Feedback** in general is information about reactions to communications, a deliverable or a situation; coaching, mentoring and negotiating are examples.

**Across the project: Monitor Communications.** This process ensures that the **information needs** of the project and its stakeholders **are met**; its key benefit is the **optimal information flow** defined in the communications and stakeholder engagement plans.

| Tools and techniques | Outputs |
|---|---|
| Expert judgment; project management information system; **stakeholder engagement assessment matrix**; **observation/conversation**; meetings | Work performance information; **change requests**; updates to the communications and stakeholder engagement plans, issue log, lessons learned register and stakeholder register |

> **On the exam.** A stakeholder says they never received important information, although it was emailed. Push communication ensures distribution, not understanding. Check the communication requirements and the feedback loop, and adjust the method in the communications management plan.

## Transparency and collaboration

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Communications management plan; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Visual controls; Agile Practice Guide, 5.5.4; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.5

The enabler **promote transparency and collaboration** is served by making information visible and current:

- **Visual controls** (information radiators in lean environments) show the process so anyone can compare actual against expected performance at a glance: for example **task boards** of work to do, in progress and done. They should be **highly visible to everyone**.
- The Agile Practice Guide's measurement guidelines: **start small** with a minimal set of metrics; **visualize openly** on information radiators or dashboards and encourage **questions rather than judgments**; review metrics in retrospectives and act; **stop collecting** metrics that no longer influence decisions.
- For remote teams: **digital kanban boards and dashboards**, kept current and used in meetings.
- The communications management plan itself helps keep all parties informed and the project **transparent**.

## Reporting requirements and reports

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.5; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Project reporting; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Status report; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Work performance reports; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Project dashboards; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Presentations; Agile Practice Guide, 5.5.5

Two enablers: **understand reporting requirements** and **create reports aligned with sponsor and stakeholder expectations**.

**Manage Communications** ensures the timely and appropriate collection, creation, distribution, storage, retrieval, management, monitoring and disposition of project information; it includes choosing the right technologies and methods and adjusting them as needs change.

**Project reporting** is collecting and distributing project information, **adapted to the level, format and detail each type of stakeholder needs**, from a simple message to custom reports and presentations, **regularly or by exception**.

| Report | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Status report** | The current status of the project: progress since the last report and **forecasts** for cost and schedule |
| **Work performance reports** | Work performance information compiled to generate **decisions, actions or awareness**, circulated as the communications plan defines. Examples: status and progress reports; earned value graphs, trend lines and forecasts, **iteration burndown charts**, defect histograms, risk summaries |
| **Project dashboard** | Charts and graphs of progress against important measures, with high-level summaries and **drill-down**. Often stoplight (**red-amber-green**) charts, bar, pie and control charts, with a text explanation for measures **outside thresholds** |
| **Presentation** | Formal delivery of information: progress reports, background for decisions, general information to raise the project's profile |

**Reporting to sponsors in agile** (Agile Practice Guide): sponsors still need to know when significant scope will be finished and how confident the team is. Stable throughput plus an up-to-date item count allows **probabilistic forecasts** ("an 85% chance of delivering the remaining 45 stories within 10 iterations"). Present forecasts as **ranges or confidence curves, never as single-point commitments**.

> **On the exam.** The sponsor wants a one-page view; the team wants task detail. Same data, different reports. If an indicator is red, say so with an explanation; a report that hides bad news is never the right answer.

## Reporting and governance

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.3; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.5.1; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.5.2; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.6.7

The last enabler is to **support reporting and governance processes**.

- Governance includes **reporting to support informed decision-making**, and builds in **feedback loops** for periodic reviews, lessons learned and adjustments. It is tailored: lightweight for adaptive methods, moderate for hybrid, comprehensive for large predictive work.
- Effective governance needs three things: **target metrics** aligned with strategic goals; clear **signaling mechanisms** or alarms for those metrics, typically **leading indicators**; and **feedback mechanisms** so decision-makers can learn from their decisions.
- **Monitor and Control Project Performance** tracks, reviews and **reports** overall progress against the plan. It provides information to stakeholders, demonstrates accountability, and gives forecasts. Changes to baselines go through integrated change control.

| | Leading indicators | Lagging indicators |
|---|---|---|
| **What they show** | Upcoming changes or trends, before variances cross the tolerance threshold | Deliverables or events, after the fact |
| **Examples** | Items in progress in the backlog; no risk management process; stakeholders not available or engaged; poorly defined success criteria | Deliverables completed; schedule or cost variance; resources consumed |
| **Use** | **Preferred whenever possible**: they help prevent problems | Easier to measure; can reveal correlations, such as schedule variance with team dissatisfaction |

Escalation paths and thresholds are part of governance (module 3.1); the communications management plan records the **escalation processes** for communication.

## Exam tips

- Plan communication from requirements: who needs what, when, in what form, and why.
- Channels: N × (N − 1) / 2. Watch "total" versus "added".
- Push does not guarantee understanding; feedback does.
- Tailor reports to the audience; report bad news promptly, with context.
- Leading indicators warn early and are preferred; lagging indicators confirm what happened.
