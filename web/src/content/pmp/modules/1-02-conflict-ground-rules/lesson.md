## Key points

- Conflict is **inevitable** on projects. Handled well, differences of opinion bring more creativity and better decisions; handled badly, they hurt productivity and relationships.
- Common **sources**: scarce resources, scheduling priorities, personal work styles; on projects, also many organizations and stakeholders trying to influence the work.
- Team members try to resolve a conflict **themselves first**. If it escalates, the project manager helps. Address it **early**, usually **in private**, with a **direct, collaborative** approach. Formal or disciplinary steps come only if disruptive conflict continues.
- Five techniques: **withdraw/avoid, smooth/accommodate, compromise/reconcile, force/direct, collaborate/problem-solve**. Choose by the situation; collaboration is the one that can give win-win.
- **Ground rules** are expectations about acceptable behavior. They are written in the **team charter**, which the team builds together and everyone shares responsibility for following.
- A broken ground rule is first handled by pointing back to what the team agreed, privately and early; the charter is reviewed and updated as the team learns.

## Where conflict comes from

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Conflict management; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.6; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.8

The first enabler is to **identify conflict sources**.

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| **Scarce resources** | Two teams need the same specialist in the same week |
| **Scheduling priorities** | Engineering wants to finish testing; marketing wants an earlier launch |
| **Personal work styles** | One member plans everything; another prefers to experiment |
| **Many parties with influence** | Projects bring together organizations, departments and vendors who do not usually work together, and many managers and executives try to influence the project. The Eighth Edition says this often creates **confusion and conflict** |
| **Diverse teams** | Internal staff, contractors, volunteers and short-term members bring different perspectives. Honoring that diversity helps conflicts be **managed efficiently** |

**Prevention comes first.** Team ground rules, group norms and solid practices such as **communication planning** and **role definition** reduce the amount of conflict.

## Reading the context of a conflict

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Conflict management

The second enabler is to **analyze the context for the conflict** before acting. The Eighth Edition lists the factors that shape which method fits:

- the **importance and intensity** of the conflict,
- the **time pressure** for resolving it,
- the **relative power** of the people involved,
- how important it is to **keep a good relationship**, and
- whether the aim is a **long-term or short-term** resolution.

It also sets out who acts, and when:

| Stage | Who | How |
|---|---|---|
| Differences become a negative factor | **Team members** | They are initially responsible for resolving it |
| The conflict escalates | **Project manager** | Helps facilitate a satisfactory resolution: early, usually in private, direct and collaborative |
| Disruptive conflict continues | Project manager, with the organization | **Formal procedures**, which may include disciplinary action |

> **On the exam.** The first step in most PMP conflict questions is to **understand the situation**: talk to the people involved, privately, to learn what the conflict is really about. Answers that escalate at once, involve the sponsor, or impose a solution before the facts are known are usually wrong unless the scenario is an emergency.

## Choosing and applying a resolution strategy

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Conflict management; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership

The third enabler is to **implement an agreed-on resolution strategy**. The five general techniques each have their place:

| Technique | What it does | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| **Withdraw / avoid** | Retreats from the conflict or postpones it, to be better prepared or for others to resolve | The issue stays open for now |
| **Smooth / accommodate** | Stresses agreement over difference; concedes to keep harmony and the relationship | The relationship is kept; the issue may remain |
| **Compromise / reconcile** | Looks for some satisfaction for all, resolving the conflict partly or for a time | Occasionally **lose-lose** |
| **Force / direct** | Pushes one view at the expense of others, usually from a position of power, to **resolve an emergency** | **Win-lose** |
| **Collaborate / problem-solve** | Brings in several viewpoints through a cooperative attitude and open dialogue | Consensus and commitment; can be **win-win** |

**How to keep a conflict from escalating** beyond useful debate (Eighth Edition, under leadership):

- **Keep communication open and respectful.** Without a safe environment, people stop communicating; keep words, tone and body language nonthreatening.
- **Focus on the issues, not the people.** The aim is to resolve the situation, not to cast blame.
- **Focus on the present and future, not the past.** Bringing up old cases intensifies the current one.
- **Search for alternatives together.** This moves the conflict into a problem-solving space.

**"Agreed-on"** means the team already knows how conflicts will be handled. The **team charter** includes **conflict resolution processes**, so the strategy is not invented in the heat of the moment.

> **On the exam.** Collaborate/problem-solve is the usual best answer when there is time and the relationship matters. Force is right only when something must be decided at once (safety, an emergency). Smoothing or withdrawing can be right as a **temporary** step: for example, cooling down before a proper conversation.

## Communicating conflict management principles

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Team charter; Agile Practice Guide, 5.1.2; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.6

The fourth enabler is to **communicate conflict management principles with the team and external stakeholders**.

- **Inside the team:** the team charter sets out communication guidelines, decision-making criteria and **conflict resolution processes**. Discussing them early lets members discover the values that matter to one another, and early commitment to clear guidelines **reduces misunderstandings**.
- **With external parties:** the Eighth Edition's example for the accountable leader principle is a government megaproject where **vendor teams** clash over shift rotations. The conventional response enforces each contract and leaves each vendor to calm its own staff. The principle instead holds **cross-vendor discussions to explore the root cause** and find acceptable adjustments. That resolves the conflict and removes friction that could have hurt quality and productivity.

> **On the exam.** When a conflict involves a vendor or another department, the PMP answer still favors bringing the parties together to find the cause and a shared solution, while respecting the contract. Pointing at contract clauses is a later step, not the first.

## Ground rules and the team charter

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Ground rules; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Team charter; Agile Practice Guide, 5.1.2; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.8

The fifth enabler is to **establish an environment that fosters adherence to common ground rules**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ground rules** | Expectations regarding acceptable behavior by project team members. Defined in the team charter; they also set expected behavior toward other stakeholders |
| **Team charter** | A document that establishes the team's values, agreements and operating guidelines |
| **Team agreements** | Behavioral parameters and working norms set by the team and upheld through individual and team commitment; created **at the beginning** of the project |

**What a team charter may contain** (Eighth Edition): team values, communication guidelines, decision-making criteria and processes, **conflict resolution processes**, meeting guidelines, and team agreements.

**What agile teams put in it** (Agile Practice Guide): team values such as sustainable pace and core hours; working agreements such as what "ready" and "done" mean, respecting the timebox, WIP limits; ground rules such as one person talking at a time in a meeting; group norms such as how meeting times are treated. The guide calls the team charter the team's **social contract**.

**What makes ground rules stick:**

- The charter **works best when the team develops it**, or at least has a chance to contribute.
- **All team members share responsibility** for making sure its rules are followed, not only the project manager.
- It is **reviewed and updated periodically**, to keep the shared understanding and to bring **new team members** in.

## Psychological safety: the ground for open disagreement

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.2.4; Agile Practice Guide, 4.2.5; Agile Practice Guide, 4.2.7

Ground rules work only where people feel safe to raise a problem. **Psychological safety** is a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking: speaking up, asking questions, admitting mistakes and suggesting changes without fear.

| Threatens it | Builds it |
|---|---|
| Shaming or punishing people for speaking up; public criticism; blame | Working agreements **defined together**, for respectful dialogue, shared accountability and inclusion |
| Hierarchical or cultural barriers that make input seem unwelcome | Leaders modeling vulnerability: admitting uncertainty and mistakes, asking for help |
| High pressure and deadlines that make people hold back concerns | Retrospectives that seek learning, not blame |
| Dropping reflection, such as retrospectives | Anonymous surveys when concerns are not voiced |
| Remote work that hides tone and weakens informal check-ins | Regular check-ins and inclusive facilitation for remote members |

The way a leader responds to disagreement **sets the tone**: constructive, nondefensive responses signal that learning matters more than being right.

## When a ground rule is broken

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Team charter; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Conflict management; Agile Practice Guide, Table 4-1

The sixth enabler is to **manage and rectify ground rule violations**. The books give the parts; put together, they make a sequence:

1. **Address it early and privately**, with a direct, collaborative conversation, as for any conflict.
2. **Point back to the charter.** The rule is the team's own agreement, so the conversation is about what everyone agreed, not about the project manager's preference.
3. **Let the team own it.** All team members share responsibility for the rules; in agile teams, the retrospective is where the team **updates its working agreements**.
4. **Review the rule itself.** If a rule is broken often, it may be unclear or no longer fit; the charter is meant to be reviewed and updated.
5. **Escalate only if disruptive behavior continues**: formal procedures, which may include disciplinary action.

> **On the exam.** A team member repeatedly breaks a ground rule (arrives late, interrupts, ignores the definition of done). The best first answer is usually a **private conversation** that refers to the team charter. Raising it publicly in a meeting, reporting it to the functional manager, or rewriting the charter alone are poor first steps.

## Exam tips

- Sources: resources, priorities, work styles; on projects, many parties with influence.
- Before choosing a technique, read the context: importance, time pressure, power, relationship, long or short term.
- Team members first; then the project manager; early, private, direct, collaborative; formal steps last.
- Collaborate when you can, force only in an emergency, compromise may be lose-lose.
- Ground rules live in the **team charter**, made by the team, owned by everyone, reviewed over time.
