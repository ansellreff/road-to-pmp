## Key points

- A **stakeholder** is anyone impacted by the project, or who **feels they may be** impacted: individuals, groups and organizations, inside and outside the organization, supporters, neutrals and opponents. The team members are stakeholders too.
- **Identify Stakeholders** is done **early and then regularly** throughout the project. Continuous identification works as a **risk management strategy**: a stakeholder found late is a risk realized.
- The **stakeholder register** holds identification information, assessment information (requirements, expectations, influence, the phase of most impact) and classification.
- **Stakeholder analysis** records positions, roles, stakes, **expectations**, **attitudes** (level of support) and interest in information. Stakes can be interest, rights, ownership, knowledge or contribution.
- **Categorizing**: power/interest and similar grids for small, simple projects; the **salience model** (power, urgency, legitimacy) for large, complex communities; the stakeholder cube; directions of influence (upward, downward, outward, sideward).
- Grid strategies: high power and high interest, **manage closely**; high power, low interest, **keep satisfied**; low power, high interest, **keep informed**; low power, low interest, **monitor**.

## Who stakeholders are

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 2-31; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.1

The Stakeholders performance domain runs **from identifying stakeholders to monitoring their engagement**, over the whole life cycle. The sponsor, the team members and **all the people impacted by, or who feel they may be impacted by,** the project are stakeholders.

- **Internal and external**: individuals, groups and organizations. Examples in the Eighth Edition: project manager, project management team, project team, sponsor, governing bodies, PMOs, steering committees, suppliers and vendors, customers, end users, regulatory bodies, local communities, even family.
- **Numbers vary**: from a small group to potentially **millions**.
- **They change**: different stakeholders may appear in different phases, and their influence, power or interest may change as the project unfolds.
- **Every attitude counts**: identification, analysis and engagement cover supporters, neutrals and **opponents**.
- **Interpersonal and leadership skills** for working with stakeholders are "just as important, if not more so" than technical project management skills.

**Key stakeholders named in the domain:**

| Stakeholder | Why they matter |
|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Holds decision-making authority, secures resources and keeps the project aligned with strategy. Initiates the project, approves the charter and the plan and their changes, assigns and empowers the project manager, and authorizes completion. **An active sponsor is a critical success factor.** |
| **Project team** | "Critical stakeholders and should be treated as such": the work and the recommendations on hard decisions come from them |
| **Vendors and suppliers** | Important stakeholders when external resources are involved |

## Identifying stakeholders

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.1; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 2-33; The PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.1.2

**Identify Stakeholders** is the process of identifying stakeholders **regularly** and analyzing and documenting their **interests, involvement, interdependencies, influence and potential impact** on project success. Its key benefit is that the team knows **the right focus for engaging each stakeholder or group**. It is performed **periodically throughout the project**, and continuous identification can work as a **risk management strategy** as the environment changes.

| Inputs (examples) | Tools and techniques | Outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Project charter; business case and benefits management plan; communications and stakeholder engagement plans; change log, issue log, requirements; agreements | Expert judgment; questionnaires and surveys; brainstorming; stakeholder analysis; document analysis; stakeholder mapping; meetings | **Stakeholder register**; change requests; updates to the requirements, communications, risk and stakeholder engagement plans; updates to the assumption log, issue log and risk register |

**Where names come from** (The PMI Guide to Business Analysis): **brainstorming** a first list; **interviews**, for example with the sponsor or operational managers; **organizational charts**, to find every group affected; **process flows**, which show who does each step. The aim is to uncover **every** stakeholder with needs to meet: overlooking a single role can produce a solution that fails hundreds or thousands of users.

> **On the exam.** Halfway through the project, a group nobody listed (a regulator, a works council, a team in another country) appears. Add it to the stakeholder register, analyze it, and update the engagement plan. Stakeholder identification is never "done" after initiation.

## The stakeholder register

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Stakeholder register; The PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.1.4

The **stakeholder register** is a project document with information about stakeholders, including their **assessment and classification**.

| Part | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Identification information** | Name, organizational position, location and contact details, role on the project |
| **Assessment information** | Major requirements, **expectations**, potential to influence the outcomes, and the **phase of the life cycle where the stakeholder has the most influence or impact** |
| **Stakeholder classification** | Internal or external; impact, influence, power or interest; upward, downward, outward or sideward; or any other model the project manager chooses |

The register is the **most current list** of stakeholders and is maintained throughout the project. It is an input to planning engagement and communications (module 1.6), and is updated whenever stakeholders are added or change.

## Analyzing stakeholders

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Stakeholder analysis; The PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2

**Stakeholder analysis** produces a list of stakeholders with their **positions** in the organization, **roles** on the project, **stakes**, **expectations**, **attitudes** (their level of support) and their **interest in information** about the project.

**A stake can be:**

| Stake | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest** | Affected by a decision about the project or its outcomes | Staff whose jobs change |
| **Rights** | Legal rights (such as occupational health and safety) or moral rights (such as protecting historical sites or the environment) | Neighbors of a construction site |
| **Ownership** | Legal title to an asset or property | The owner of the land |
| **Knowledge** | Specialist knowledge, including of the organization's power structures | An experienced engineer |
| **Contribution** | Funds or other resources, or advocacy, or acting as a buffer between the project and the organization's politics | A supportive executive |

**Characteristics worth analyzing** (The PMI Guide to Business Analysis): **attitude** (who supports the work and who does not), **experience**, **interests** (who gains or loses from the solution) and **level of influence**. Analysis is **iterative**: it is revisited as stakeholders are discovered or change. With many stakeholders, **group** them by common characteristics.

## Categorizing stakeholders

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Stakeholder mapping/representation; The Project Management Answer Book, Chapter 13, Q14; The PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.2.4

Categorizing helps the team build relationships with stakeholders. The outline's enabler **categorize stakeholders** maps to these methods:

| Method | Groups stakeholders by | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Power/interest grid** (also power/influence, impact/influence) | Authority (power), concern about outcomes (interest), ability to influence outcomes (influence), or ability to cause changes to planning or execution (impact) | **Small projects**, simple relationships |
| **Stakeholder cube** | The grid elements combined in **three dimensions** | A richer, multidimensional picture; communication strategies |
| **Salience model** | **Power** (authority or ability to influence), **urgency** (need for immediate attention, from time pressure or high stakes) and **legitimacy** (their involvement is appropriate). A variant uses **proximity** (direct involvement in the work) instead of legitimacy | **Large, complex communities** with complex networks of relationships; finding **relative importance** |
| **Directions of influence** | **Upward** (senior management, sponsor, steering committee); **downward** (the team, temporary specialists); **outward** (suppliers, government, public, end users, regulators); **sideward** (the project manager's peers competing for or sharing resources) | Seeing where each stakeholder acts on the project |
| **Prioritization** | Ranking | Many stakeholders, frequently changing membership, or complex relationships |

**The power/interest grid and its strategies:**

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | **Keep satisfied** | **Manage closely** |
| **Low power** | **Monitor** | **Keep informed** |

A practical use: add a "stakeholder strategy" column to the register and fill it in from the grid.

**From business analysis:** a **stakeholder matrix** can use **influence** (on requirements) against **impact** (of the solution on them). Low influence but high impact stakeholders, such as the people who will use the solution every day, are critical sources of requirements even without power. An **onion diagram** places stakeholders in rings by how close their relationship to the solution is: those building it at the center, external stakeholders outside.

> **On the exam.** Low power does not mean low priority. Stakeholders with high interest and low power still need to be kept informed, and those the outcome affects most may hold the requirements that make or break the product.

## Finding each stakeholder's expectations

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.1; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Stakeholder register; The PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.2.2

The outline's enabler **identify stakeholder expectations** belongs to People task 5. Expectations sit in the **assessment information** of the register, beside major requirements.

- **Stakeholder satisfaction** should be **prioritized and integrated into the project objectives**. The key is **continuous communication** with everyone (customers, end users, managers, executives, team members) to understand their **needs and expectations**, address issues as they occur, manage conflicting interests, and involve them in decisions.
- The **sponsor**, often an executive, needs specific effort to understand their needs and the way of communicating that suits them.
- **Personas** help with large groups of users: a persona is a **fictional character representing a class of users**, with goals, behaviors and motivations. Unlike a stakeholder, a persona may have no interest in the outcome and no influence; it helps the team understand how a group will use the product.

Facilitating discussions to **align** expectations (the rest of People task 5) is in module 1.7; engaging stakeholders to meet them is module 1.6.

## Predictive and agile

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.3.1; The PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.4.2; Agile Practice Guide, 5.2.6

| | Predictive | Agile |
|---|---|---|
| When stakeholders are identified | Early, from the charter and business documents, then reviewed at least when the project moves through phases | Early, then again whenever refinements to the product scope add or remove stakeholders |
| Who works with them | The project manager, through the register and the engagement plan | The **product owner** works with stakeholders, customers and managers and ranks the work by business value |
| How their expectations are learned | Requirements documentation and interviews; formal updates such as newsletters and town hall meetings in hierarchical organizations | **Continuous feedback** on features and stories: reviews of working product at least every two weeks |

## Exam tips

- Identify stakeholders early, and keep identifying them: a late discovery goes into the register and the plan straight away.
- The register: who they are, what they need and expect, how much they can influence, and how they are classified.
- Grid for small and simple; salience for large and complex.
- Manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor. Low power is not low priority.
- An active sponsor is a critical success factor; the team are stakeholders too.
