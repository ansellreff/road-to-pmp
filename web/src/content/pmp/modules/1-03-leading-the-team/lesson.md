## Key points

- **Lead the Team** is the Eighth Edition process for guiding, developing and managing the team through feedback, collaboration, conflict resolution or escalation. It combines **management** (processes, planning, measuring) and **leadership** (influencing, motivating, listening, enabling).
- **Situational leadership**: adapt the style to the team's needs, the time sensitivity of the task and the complexity of the project; more directive in critical moments, more supportive when autonomy helps.
- In agile settings the project manager is a **supportive (servant) leader**: purpose first, then people, then process; facilitate rather than decide for others; remove organizational impediments; pave the way for the team.
- **Empowered** teams decide how they do their work. People who are micromanaged perform worse and are less satisfied.
- **Problem-solving** follows steps: define, find the root cause, generate options, choose, implement, verify. Decisions beyond the team's authority are escalated **with options and their impacts**.
- **Roles** must be clear: one accountable person per task in a responsibility assignment matrix; in agile teams, the lightweight **RAF** model (responsible, accountable, facilitator) where confusion exists.

## What leading the team involves

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership

| | Focus | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Management activities** | The means of meeting objectives | Effective processes, planning, coordinating, measuring and monitoring work |
| **Leadership activities** | People | Influencing, motivating, listening, enabling |

Leadership, in the Eighth Edition's words, is the knowledge, skills and behaviors needed to **guide, motivate and direct a team**. It includes negotiation, resilience, communication, problem-solving, critical thinking and interpersonal skills. "People can be counted, but they are not numbers."

A project manager should be **sensitive to the willingness and ability** of team members to do their work, and adjust management and leadership styles accordingly.

## Setting expectations at the team level

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.1; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Team charter; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.8

The first enabler is to **establish expectations at the team level**. The Eighth Edition lists what every team development effort covers:

| Aspect | What the leader does |
|---|---|
| **Vision and objectives** | Communicates them throughout and refers to them in decisions (module 1.1) |
| **Roles and responsibilities** | Makes sure each member understands and fulfills their role; finds gaps in knowledge and skills and closes them with training, mentoring or coaching |
| **Team operations** | Facilitates communication, problem-solving and consensus, for example by building a **team charter** and team norms with the team |
| **Guidance** | Keeps the whole team headed in the right direction |
| **Growth** | Points out what goes well and what can improve; sets improvement goals with the team; helps individuals develop |

An empowered culture rests on **full clarity** about individual roles, responsibilities, team agreements and guiding processes; that clarity is what lets people work together and build trust.

## Choosing a leadership style

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 2.5.1.1; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.6; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Centralized management and leadership; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Distributed management and leadership

The enabler **determine an appropriate leadership style** has a clear answer in the Eighth Edition: there is no single right style. Leaders should be **versatile** and able to switch styles to get a better outcome. **Situational leadership** is named as particularly relevant: adjust to the specific needs of the team, the **time sensitivity** of tasks and the **complexity** of the project, for example **more directive in critical phases, supportive when team autonomy is beneficial**.

Accountable leaders show **flexibility and adaptability**: they adapt their style to the situation and the audience **without losing their core values**.

**How decisions and authority are arranged:**

| Structure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Centralized management and leadership** | Top-down: decision-making and authority sit with top management, which instructs other levels |
| **Distributed management and leadership** | Decision-making and authority are spread among individuals and teams; the team may self-organize, and someone in the team may act as facilitator, a role that can move between members |

Whatever the structure, **leadership activities** should be practiced by **all** team members. Leadership is not the same as **authority**: authority is a position of control given by the organization; leadership inspires and motivates by example. **Shared leadership** is normal: at different moments a team member, a stakeholder or a specialist may lead.

> **On the exam.** A new team that lacks experience, a safety incident, or an urgent deadline justify a more directive style for a time. A skilled, self-managing team calls for a supportive style. The wrong answer is usually the one that keeps one style for every situation.

## Supportive leadership and empowering the team

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Servant leadership; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.3; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.4.1; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 4.2

The enabler **empower the team** is where the PMBOK Guide and the Agile Practice Guide meet.

**Servant leadership** (Eighth Edition) is leading the team by focusing on understanding and addressing the **needs and development of team members**, to enable the highest possible team performance, in service of the project's business objectives. The Agile Practice Guide, second edition, calls the same practice **supportive leadership**. Its order of work is:

1. **Purpose**: define the "why" with the team; the team optimizes at the project level, not the person level.
2. **People**: create an environment where everyone can succeed; ask each member to contribute.
3. **Process**: look for results, not for a "perfect" agile process.

**Characteristics of supportive leadership:** promoting self-awareness; listening; serving those on the team; helping people grow; **coaching versus controlling**; promoting safety, respect and trust; promoting the energy and intelligence of others.

**What empowerment looks like:**

- In an agile project the project manager **shifts from the center** of coordination to **supporting** the team: coaching, fostering collaboration, aligning stakeholder needs, and **distributing responsibility to the people who have the knowledge** to do the work.
- Agile teams are **self-managing**: members decide who will do the work within the next period's scope.
- Among the marks of high-performing teams, the Eighth Edition lists **empowerment, delegation and autonomy**: members who may decide how they work perform better and are more satisfied than those who are **micromanaged**. It also lists **recognition**: even simple appreciation reinforces good behavior.

> **Edition note.** The first edition of the Agile Practice Guide (2017) called this **servant leadership** ("Servant Leadership Empowers the Team"). The second edition says **supportive leadership**, with the same definition. PMP questions may use either name.

## Representing the voice of the team

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.1; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.3; PMBOK 8, Standard 3.6

The enabler **represent the voice of the team** is about the leader's work **outside** the team, on its behalf.

| Responsibility (Agile Practice Guide) | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Facilitate** | Help everyone do their best thinking; be an impartial bridge builder and coach, not the one who makes decisions others should make |
| **Remove organizational impediments** | Review processes that slow the team (extensive documentation, change boards, audits, finance, release queues) and work with those departments to streamline them |
| **Pave the way for others' contributions** | Arrange a team space, keep the team focused on one project, work with auditors or finance on processes that fit agile work |
| **Educate stakeholders** | Explain why and how to be agile, and the value of empowered teams |
| **Connect and enable** | On larger efforts: coordinate across teams and vendors, align goals with business outcomes, secure resources, manage risks, keep sponsors informed, escalate organization-level impediments |

An accountable leader also **engages and influences stakeholders and sponsors** to secure the support the project needs.

> **On the exam.** If the team is blocked by something outside its control (a slow approval, a missing tool, a department's rule), the project manager's job is to work with whoever owns that obstacle, not to tell the team to work around it or to wait.

## Solving problems

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Problem-solving; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.1

The enabler **solve problems**:

**Steps** (Eighth Edition): define the problem; identify the root cause; generate possible solutions; choose the best; implement it; verify that it works.

**Deciding together.** The Eighth Edition weighs the ways decisions are made:

| Way | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Unilateral** | Fast | More errors than drawing on a diverse group; can demotivate those affected |
| **Group-based** | Uses the group's knowledge; increases buy-in even when the choice was not everyone's first | Takes time and interrupts the work |
| **Diverge / converge** | Many options first, often generated individually so senior or charismatic people do not sway others; then the team converges. Roman voting, wideband Delphi and fist of five follow this pattern and reduce **groupthink** | Needs facilitation |

**Beyond the team's authority:** the team investigates alternatives, considers the impact of each, and **escalates the decision** to someone with the authority, "do not bring me problems, bring me solutions", within the organization's governance.

A supportive leader also helps the team find **the root causes of recurrent problems**, and helps with project management techniques the team lacks, such as quantitative risk analysis.

## Supporting varied experiences, skills and perspectives

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 3.8; Agile Practice Guide, 4.2.5; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Emotional intelligence; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership; Agile Practice Guide, 4.6; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.1

The enabler **support the team's varied experiences, skills and perspectives**:

- **Diversity** enriches the team with different perspectives; a team that honors diversity and **harnesses it constructively** manages conflict efficiently (Eighth Edition, empowered culture).
- **Amplify quieter voices** and make space for underrepresented views, so everyone is heard; this also improves decisions by widening the input (Agile Practice Guide).
- **Emotional intelligence**: identify, assess and manage your own emotions and those of others. Four areas: **self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, social skills**. It reduces tension, increases cooperation, and is a basis of all forms of leadership.
- **Skills**: agile teams mix specialists and generalists; many members become **generalizing specialists** with **T-shaped skills** (one specialty plus breadth), which lets them help one another and avoid bottlenecks. **I-shaped** people have deep skill in one area only.
- **Growth**: build capability through mentoring, encouragement and training, "even if that means losing them from the team".

## Clear roles and responsibilities

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Responsibility assignment matrix; Agile Practice Guide, 4.4.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.4.3; Agile Practice Guide, 4.5

The last enabler is to **establish clear roles and responsibilities within the team**.

**Predictive:** a **responsibility assignment matrix** shows the people assigned to each work package or activity, at several levels on larger projects. It ensures **only one person is accountable** for any task.

**Agile roles** (Agile Practice Guide): cross-functional, self-organizing **team members**; the **product owner**, who guides the product's direction and ranks work by business value; and the **team facilitator** (supportive leader; may be called project manager, scrum master, team lead or coach).

**Two extremes to avoid** when agile roles are unclear:

| Extreme | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| **Full autonomy**, no role definitions at all | Diffusion of responsibility, bloated meetings, unclear decision rights, uncertainty about contributions |
| **Rigid role frameworks** layered on old structures | Bureaucratic handoffs, overlaps, renamed roles with no new purpose, several people owning the same outcome |

**The RAF model** (new in the second edition) is a lightweight alternative to RACI, applied **only where confusion exists**:

| Letter | Who |
|---|---|
| **R**esponsible | Actively performs the work |
| **A**ccountable | Owns the outcome or decision |
| **F**acilitator | Supports the process, resolves blockers, keeps momentum |

Guidelines: focus on activities, not titles; apply selectively; adapt to context; emphasize outcomes; review in retrospectives; retire definitions that no longer help. Clear roles bring faster decisions, fewer handoffs, less duplicated effort, and **psychological safety**, because people know what is expected of them.

## What high-performing teams have

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.2

Open communication; shared understanding of the purpose; shared ownership; trust; collaboration rather than silos; adaptability; resilience; empowerment, delegation and autonomy; recognition. These are the goal of effective leadership, and the reasons behind most correct answers in this module.

## Exam tips

- Style follows the situation: directive when it is critical, supportive when the team can run itself.
- Servant (supportive) leader: purpose, people, process; coach, do not control; remove impediments; shield and represent the team.
- Empower: let the team decide how; distribute responsibility to those with the knowledge.
- Problems: root cause before solution; escalate decisions beyond your authority **with options**.
- Roles: one accountable per task; in agile, RAF where confusion exists.
