## Key points

- The **Lead the Team** process (module 1.3) has a set of tools for raising team performance: team development models, motivation, recognition, emotional and cultural intelligence, colocation and virtual teams, coaching, training, assessments, retrospectives and problem-solving methods. Its output is **team performance assessments**.
- **Tuckman ladder**: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning. Teams can get stuck, slip back to an earlier stage, or skip one if they have worked together before.
- **Motivation**: people are motivated by different things. Herzberg: hygiene factors (salary, policies, environment) only prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, growth) create satisfaction. Pink: once pay is fair, **autonomy, mastery and purpose** matter most for complex work.
- **Recognition** works best **throughout** the project, tailored to the person and their culture; intangible rewards can matter as much as money.
- **Virtual teams** widen the talent pool and cut costs, but need more communication planning: clear expectations, conflict protocols, inclusion in decisions, cultural understanding, shared credit.
- **Retrospectives** are regular lessons learned workshops, at least at the end of every iteration; blame-free, with a few improvements the team can really finish.

## How a team develops: the Tuckman ladder

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Tuckman ladder; Agile Practice Guide, 6.3.3

The **Tuckman ladder** is one model of team development. It has **five stages**:

| Stage | What happens | Leader's focus (from modules 1.1 to 1.3) |
|---|---|---|
| **Forming** | Members meet and learn about the project and their formal roles. They tend to be independent and not very open | Clear roles, the vision, a team charter |
| **Storming** | The team starts on the work, technical decisions and the management approach. Without collaboration and openness to other views, the climate can turn counterproductive | Facilitating disagreement, ground rules (module 1.2) |
| **Norming** | Members begin to work together and adjust their habits to support the team. They learn to **trust** one another | Reinforcing the team's own agreements |
| **Performing** | The team works as a well-organized unit: **interdependent**, working through issues smoothly | Empowering and staying out of the way (module 1.3) |
| **Adjourning** | The work is completed and people move on, often as deliverables finish and staff are released | Recognition, lessons learned, a planned release |

Three facts the exam likes:

- The stages usually come **in order**, but a team can **get stuck** in one stage or **regress** to an earlier one.
- A team whose members **worked together before** may **skip** a stage.
- How long each stage lasts depends on **team dynamics, team size and team leadership**. The project management team should understand team dynamics well enough to move people through every stage.

**Changes to the team restart the climb.** The Agile Practice Guide notes that adding, removing, merging or switching members (**reteaming**) can bring new energy, but teams then need time to re-establish working agreements, clarify roles and reconnect to shared goals as they move through the Tuckman stages again.

> **On the exam.** A new member joins a performing team and arguments break out about how work is done: the team has slipped back to **storming**. The best answer helps the team revisit its agreements and roles; it does not blame the newcomer or treat the arguments as a disaster.

## Motivation

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Motivation; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership

**Motivation** is providing a reason for someone to act. Leading the team has two sides to it: understanding **what** motivates each person, and working with people so they **stay committed** to the project and its outcomes. Teams are motivated by being **empowered to take part in decisions** and encouraged to work independently. Because people are motivated by **different things**, understanding them lets the leader **tailor rewards to the individual**.

The Eighth Edition describes four models, "a small representation" of many:

| Model | Idea | What it means for the project manager |
|---|---|---|
| **Hygiene and motivational factors** (Herzberg) | **Motivational factors** concern the content of the work: achievement, growth, advancement. Enough of them leads to satisfaction. **Hygiene factors** concern conditions: company policies, salary, physical environment. Too little causes dissatisfaction, but even enough **does not create satisfaction** | Fix poor conditions to remove complaints, but do not expect a pay rise or new desks to motivate. Interesting, challenging work does |
| **Intrinsic versus extrinsic motivation** (Pink) | Extrinsic rewards such as salary motivate only up to a point: once pay is fair, their power stops. For complicated, challenging work, **intrinsic** motivators last longer and work better: **autonomy** (how, where and when to work), **mastery** (to improve and excel), **purpose** (to make a difference) | Give self-management, room to learn, and a clear link between each task and the vision |
| **Theory of needs** (McClelland) | Everyone is driven by needs for **achievement**, **power** and **affiliation**, in different strengths depending on experience and culture | Achievers want challenging but reasonable goals; power-motivated people want more responsibility; affiliation-motivated people want to belong to a team |
| **Theory X, Y and Z** (McGregor; Maslow; Ouchi) | **X**: people work only for income; managed hands-on and top-down. **Y**: people want to do good work; managed with a coaching style that encourages creativity. **Z**: Maslow's version is motivation by self-realization, values and a higher calling; Ouchi's is a job for life focused on employees' well-being | Knowledge work usually calls for a Theory Y style; Theory X fits labor-intensive settings with many management layers |

Some emotional intelligence models add **motivation** as a fifth area: understanding what drives and inspires people.

> **On the exam.** A skilled developer is unhappy although well paid. Offering more money targets an extrinsic, hygiene-type factor. The better answer gives more autonomy, a chance to grow, or a clearer link to the purpose of the work.

## Recognition and rewards

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Recognition and rewards; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.2; PMBOK 6, 9.4.2.5

Recognizing and rewarding desirable behavior is part of team development. The **first plan** for rewards is made in **Plan Resource Management**.

- People are motivated when they feel **valued**, and rewards show that value.
- **Money** is the tangible part of a reward system, but **intangible rewards** can be equally or even more effective. Most team members want the chance to **grow, accomplish, be appreciated**, and use their skills on new challenges.
- **Cultural differences** change how recognition is best given; tailoring it to cultural preferences makes the reward system work better.
- Give recognition **throughout the project**, not only at the end.

Recognition is also one of the marks of a high-performing team: even simple appreciation reinforces positive behavior.

The Sixth Edition put the test plainly: a reward is effective **only if it satisfies a need that the individual values**.

> **On the exam.** Before choosing a reward, find out what the person values. A public award may delight one team member and embarrass another; a training course may matter more than a gift card. "The same reward for everyone, at the end" is rarely the best answer.

## Emotional and cultural intelligence

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Emotional intelligence; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Leadership; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Organizational cultural intelligence

Module 1.3 introduced **emotional intelligence** and its four areas. As a tool for leading the team, the Eighth Edition says more:

- It covers your own emotions, other people's, and the **collective emotions of groups**.
- A team uses it to **reduce tension and increase cooperation**: identifying and managing team members' sentiments, **anticipating** their actions, **acknowledging** their concerns, and **following up** on their issues.
- A project manager should develop both **inbound** competencies (self-awareness, self-management) and **outbound** ones (relationship management).
- Teams that build **team emotional intelligence** are more effective and usually see **lower staff turnover**.

**The four areas, in practice** (Figure 5-11):

| | Awareness | Management and skill |
|---|---|---|
| **Oneself** | **Self-awareness**: how do you affect the team, and how does the team affect you? | **Self-management**: think before you act; build trust |
| **Others** | **Social awareness**: be empathetic; listen actively | **Social skills**: establish rapport; build effective teams; manage attitude |

Self-awareness and self-management keep you **calm and productive in difficult circumstances**; social awareness and social skills create **better bonds** with the team and stakeholders.

**Organizational cultural intelligence** is the capability to perform effectively in **culturally diverse, complex situations**. Leaders with it recognize that people from different cultural regions may think and express themselves differently, and at the same time **avoid careless cultural stereotyping**.

## Colocated, remote and virtual teams

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Colocation; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Virtual teams; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Virtual collaboration tools; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.1; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.3; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.4; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.5

**Colocation** places team members physically close together to improve communication, working relationships and productivity.

**Virtual teams** share a goal but spend little or no time meeting face to face. They make it possible to:

- form teams of people spread over wide areas, and add experts who live elsewhere;
- include people working from home, on different shifts, or with mobility limitations or disabilities;
- go ahead with projects that travel costs would have stopped, and save office and equipment costs.

The price is **more communication planning**: extra time to set clear expectations, facilitate communication, agree **protocols for resolving conflicts**, include people in decisions, understand cultural differences, and **share credit** for successes. **Virtual collaboration tools**, such as cloud-based platforms where everyone can work on the same documents in real time, support the team.

**Terms from the Agile Practice Guide:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Distributed** teams | Cross-functional teams, each in a different location |
| **Dispersed** teams | Each member may work in a different place, office or home |
| **Remote** team | Members work from separate locations rather than a shared office |
| **Hybrid-location** team | Some members on site, some remote, using a mix of physical and virtual methods |

**Benefits and challenges of remote work:**

| Benefits | Challenges |
|---|---|
| Fewer distractions, lower costs, wider talent pools, flexibility, less commuting, more diverse perspectives | **Proximity bias** in recognition and advancement, lower cohesion and trust, security risks, blurred work and personal time, tool fatigue, harder onboarding and mentoring, time zone barriers |

**What successful remote teams do:** build trust through purpose, accountability and ownership; set **communication norms** about availability and tools; use video in meetings when possible; allow focus time and flexible schedules; create rituals such as virtual coffee chats; celebrate progress; protect work-life boundaries; and promote inclusion by **rotating meeting times across time zones**. Remote teams are measured by **outcomes**, not hours or presence: "trust replaces in-office observation."

**Agile practices adjusted:** cocreated working agreements; short daily meetings by video, or written updates for very distributed teams; digital boards for planning and as information radiators; retrospectives with whiteboards or polls, rotating facilitation and anonymous feedback if needed; recorded demos; virtual pairing.

**Part-time members.** When someone is only 25% or 50% on a team, they multitask, which lowers the team's throughput and predictability. Task-switching costs **20% to 40%** of productivity, so a person split over two projects is not 50% on each. Make part-time commitments explicit with everyone, and treat them as a risk.

> **On the exam.** In a virtual team where people in one time zone always attend at inconvenient hours, rotate the meeting time. If remote members are overlooked for recognition, that is proximity bias, and the project manager should correct it.

## Developing people: coaching, mentoring, training, team building

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Coaching and mentoring; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Training; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Team building; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.1

When the leader finds **gaps in knowledge and skills**, the Eighth Edition's answer is training, mentoring or coaching.

| Tool | What it is | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Coaching** | Guides a person to **find a solution to a specific problem on their own**, by asking the right questions | A specific goal; one-to-one |
| **Mentoring** | Shares knowledge, skills or experience with someone developing their own; a counselor or guide | A **long-term**, ongoing relationship; one-to-one |
| **Training** | All activities that enhance team members' competencies: classroom, online, computer-based, on-the-job from a colleague, mentoring, coaching | **Scheduled** training follows the resource management plan; **unplanned** training follows observation, conversation and performance appraisals |
| **Team building** | Activities that strengthen social relationships and build a collaborative environment, from a 5-minute agenda item to an off-site event | Most important at the start, but **continuous**; especially valuable for remote teams |

Training costs can sit in the project budget, or with the organization if the skills will help future projects. Skills a team member lacks can be developed **as part of the project work**.

Team building is not a one-off: changes in the project environment call for renewed efforts, and the project manager keeps monitoring how the team functions to prevent or correct problems.

## Assessing team performance

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Individual and team assessments; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.6.2.4.2

**Individual and team assessments** show strengths and weaknesses: members' preferences and aspirations, how they process information, make decisions and interact. Tools include **attitudinal surveys, specific assessments, structured interviews, ability tests and focus groups**. They improve understanding, trust, commitment and communication, and make teams more productive.

The output of Lead the Team, **team performance assessments**, judges the team against the marks of high-performing teams (module 1.3): open communication, shared understanding, shared ownership, trust, collaboration, adaptability, resilience, empowerment, recognition. The results point to the next steps: training, coaching, team building, changes to roles.

## Retrospectives and better thinking

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Retrospectives; Agile Practice Guide, 5.2.7; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Critical thinking; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Six Thinking Hats

**Retrospectives** are regular workshops in which the team explores its work and results to improve both **process and product**. They are a form of lessons learned meeting held **frequently**, at minimum at the end of each iteration, so issues are fixed now rather than at the end of the project. The term comes from agile, but it can mean any lessons learned session.

The Agile Practice Guide calls the retrospective possibly **the most important agile practice**:

- Hold one at the end of an iteration, or when a release ships, when several weeks have passed, when work is **stuck**, or at any milestone.
- It is **not about blame**: create a psychologically safe space.
- Use **qualitative** (feelings) and **quantitative** (measurements) data to find root causes and design countermeasures.
- **Limit the action items to the team's capacity**: finishing a few improvements beats starting many. Decide how to measure each one, and check the result next time.

**Critical thinking** is the ability to objectively question, analyze, interpret, evaluate and judge information to make reasonable decisions: recognizing **bias**, finding root causes, and handling ambiguity and complexity. It is disciplined, rational and evidence-based, and needs an open mind.

**Six Thinking Hats** is a group discussion technique in which each colored hat stands for one way of thinking:

| Hat | Thinking |
|---|---|
| **Blue** | Facilitates; the big picture |
| **White** | Facts and information |
| **Red** | Feelings and emotions |
| **Black** | The negative view: what could go wrong |
| **Yellow** | The positive aspects |
| **Green** | New ideas |

## Exam tips

- Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning. A change to the team can send it back to storming.
- Herzberg: hygiene factors stop dissatisfaction but do not motivate; growth and achievement do. Pink: autonomy, mastery, purpose.
- Recognize often, during the project, in a way that fits the person and the culture.
- Virtual team problems are usually communication problems: agree norms, rotate meeting times, include everyone.
- Retrospectives: regular, blame-free, a few improvements the team can finish.
