## Key points

- The **Stakeholders** domain has **seven processes**: one for identifying stakeholders, three for **engagement** and three for **communications**.
- **Identify Stakeholders** produces the **stakeholder register**. It is done early and **repeated throughout** the project.
- The **power/interest grid** tells you how much attention each stakeholder needs. High power and high interest: **manage closely**.
- The **stakeholder engagement assessment matrix** compares each stakeholder's **current (C)** and **desired (D)** engagement level: unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading.
- Communication methods: **interactive** (real-time, two-way), **push** (sent to recipients), **pull** (recipients fetch it themselves).
- Number of communication channels = **n(n − 1) ÷ 2**.
- The **sender** is responsible for a clear, complete message and for **confirming it was understood**.

## What the Stakeholders domain covers

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.1

A **stakeholder** is anyone who is impacted by the project, or who **feels they may be** impacted by it: the sponsor, the team, customers, end users, suppliers, regulators, the local community. They may be internal or external, supportive, neutral or opposed.

- Stakeholders, and their power and interest, **change during the project**, so identification and analysis are repeated.
- Interpersonal and leadership skills matter here as much as technical skills, especially **negotiation** and **conflict management**.
- **Team members are stakeholders too.**
- **Suppliers and vendors** are important stakeholders when outside resources are used.

**The sponsor** is a key stakeholder. Research shows that an **active sponsor** is a critical success factor. The sponsor typically:

- initiates the project and defines its objectives and business case,
- **approves the project charter** and the project management plan, and changes to them,
- assigns and empowers the project manager,
- secures resources and keeps the project aligned with strategy,
- can propose terminating the project, and authorizes its completion.

> **Edition note.** Older material has two separate knowledge areas: Stakeholder Management and Communications Management. The 8th edition combines them in one domain. The process names are almost unchanged.

## The seven Stakeholders processes

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 2-32

| Focus Area | Process | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| Initiating | **Identify Stakeholders** | Stakeholder register |
| Planning | **Plan Stakeholder Engagement** | Stakeholder engagement plan |
| Planning | **Plan Communications Management** | Communications management plan |
| Executing | **Manage Stakeholder Engagement** | Change requests, updates to the issue log and plans |
| Executing | **Manage Communications** | Project communications |
| Monitoring and Controlling | **Monitor Stakeholder Engagement** | Work performance information, change requests |
| Monitoring and Controlling | **Monitor Communications** | Work performance information, change requests |

Identify Stakeholders is one of only two processes in the **Initiating** Focus Area. The other is Initiate Project or Phase, which creates the charter.

## Identify Stakeholders and the stakeholder register

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.1; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Stakeholder register; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Stakeholder analysis

Identifies the people, groups and organizations with a stake in the project, and analyzes and documents their interests, involvement, influence and potential impact. It is performed **periodically throughout the project**. Missing a stakeholder early is a common cause of late changes and risk.

The output is the **stakeholder register**, a project document with three kinds of information:

| Part | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Identification** | Name, position, location, contact details, role on the project |
| **Assessment** | Main requirements and expectations, potential influence, the phase where they matter most |
| **Classification** | Internal or external; power, interest, influence, impact; direction of influence |

**Stakeholder analysis** looks at each stakeholder's position, role, expectations, attitude and "stake". A stake can be an **interest**, a legal or moral **right**, **ownership**, **knowledge** or a **contribution** such as funds or support.

## Ways to map stakeholders

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Stakeholder mapping/representation; The Project Management Answer Book, Chapter 13, Q14

| Model | How it classifies stakeholders | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Power/interest grid** (also power/influence, impact/influence) | By level of authority and level of concern about the outcome | Small projects, simple relationships |
| **Stakeholder cube** | Combines the grid elements in three dimensions | A richer picture of the same factors |
| **Salience model** | By **power**, **urgency** and **legitimacy** | Large, complex stakeholder communities |
| **Directions of influence** | **Upward** (senior management, sponsor), **downward** (the team), **outward** (suppliers, regulators, end users), **sideward** (peers of the project manager) | Seeing who must be managed in which direction |

**The power/interest grid** and the usual strategy for each corner:

| | **Low interest** | **High interest** |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | **Keep satisfied** | **Manage closely** |
| **Low power** | **Monitor** (least effort) | **Keep informed** |

## Plan Stakeholder Engagement

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.2; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Stakeholder engagement plan; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Stakeholder engagement assessment matrix

Develops strategies to engage stakeholders based on their needs, expectations, interests and potential impact. The output is the **stakeholder engagement plan**, a component of the project management plan.

The main tool is the **stakeholder engagement assessment matrix**. It compares each stakeholder's **current (C)** engagement level with the **desired (D)** level.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Does not know about the project or its impacts |
| **Resistant** | Knows about the project and resists the change; unsupportive |
| **Neutral** | Knows about the project; neither supportive nor unsupportive |
| **Supportive** | Knows about the project and supports the work and its outcomes |
| **Leading** | Actively engaged in making the project a success |

Example:

| Stakeholder | Unaware | Resistant | Neutral | Supportive | Leading |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Finance director | | C | | D | |
| Head of operations | | | C | | D |
| IT manager | | | | C D | |

- The **gap between C and D** shows where engagement effort is needed. The IT manager needs no special action.
- Not everyone needs to be "leading". For many stakeholders "supportive" is enough.
- The stakeholder engagement plan may hold **sensitive** information, so its distribution is handled with care.

## Plan Communications Management

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.3; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Communications management plan; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication requirements analysis; The Project Management Answer Book, Chapter 11, Q18

Plans how to communicate with stakeholders inside and outside the team. The output is the **communications management plan**, which answers:

- **Who** needs the information, and who sends it?
- **What** will be communicated: content, format, language, level of detail?
- **When** and how often?
- **How**: which methods and technologies?
- What is the **escalation process**, and who may release confidential information?

**Communication requirements analysis** determines the information needs of the stakeholders. One input is the number of **communication channels**, which grows quickly as people are added:

**Channels = n(n − 1) ÷ 2**, where n is the number of people.

| People | Calculation | Channels |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 4 × 3 ÷ 2 | 6 |
| 5 | 5 × 4 ÷ 2 | 10 |
| 10 | 10 × 9 ÷ 2 | 45 |

Remember to **count the project manager**. "A project manager with a team of 9" means n = 10. Going from 5 to 10 people adds 45 − 10 = **35** channels.

## Communication methods and technology

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication methods; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication technology

| Method | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Interactive** | Two or more parties exchange information **in real time**. The best way to reach a common understanding. | Meetings, phone calls, videoconferences, instant messaging |
| **Push** | **Sent** to specific recipients. It ensures the information is distributed, **not** that it is received or understood. | Emails, memos, reports, letters, voicemails, press releases |
| **Pull** | Recipients **fetch** the content themselves when they choose. For large amounts of information or large audiences. | Web portals, intranet sites, e-learning, lessons learned databases |

The choice of **communication technology** depends on: the **urgency** of the information, the **availability and reliability** of the technology, **ease of use**, the **project environment** (face-to-face or virtual, time zones, languages), and the **sensitivity and confidentiality** of the information.

## The communication model

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Communication models; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 5-2

| Step | Who | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Encode** | Sender | Turns the thought into words, images or sound |
| **Transmit** | Sender | Sends the message through a medium (channel) |
| **Decode** | Receiver | Turns the message back into meaning |
| **Acknowledge** | Receiver | Signals that the message was **received**. This is **not** agreement or understanding. |
| **Feedback / response** | Receiver | Encodes a reply and sends it back, showing what was understood |

- **Noise** is anything that interferes with understanding: distance, unfamiliar technology, distraction, language, culture, lack of background knowledge.
- The **sender** is responsible for making the message clear and complete and for **confirming it was correctly understood**.
- The **receiver** is responsible for receiving the whole message, interpreting it correctly, and acknowledging or responding.
- Both sides are influenced by their emotional state, knowledge, personality, culture and biases, which matters most in **cross-cultural** communication.
- **Active listening** (acknowledging, clarifying, confirming) is how feedback is achieved between people.

## Managing and monitoring

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.4; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.5; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.6; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.2.7

| Process | What it does | Key benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Manage Stakeholder Engagement** | Communicating and working with stakeholders to meet their needs, address issues and foster involvement | **Increases support and minimizes resistance** |
| **Manage Communications** | Collecting, creating, distributing, storing and finally disposing of project information | An efficient information flow between team and stakeholders |
| **Monitor Stakeholder Engagement** | Assessing whether engagement is working and adjusting strategies and plans | Keeps engagement effective as the project changes |
| **Monitor Communications** | Checking that the information needs of the project and its stakeholders are met | The information flow planned in the communications management plan |

- **Work performance reports** are an input to Manage Communications: this is how reports reach stakeholders.
- Stakeholder concerns are recorded and tracked in the **issue log**.
- Skills used: conflict management, negotiation, cultural awareness, political awareness, active listening, networking.
- In Monitor Stakeholder Engagement, the engagement assessment matrix is used again to see whether the gap between current and desired levels has **closed**.

## Stakeholders in adaptive projects

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.5.3

- Agile projects need **continuous feedback** on features and user stories, so stakeholders are engaged frequently and directly.
- Regular events (planning sessions, **reviews** and **retrospectives**) and daily coordination meetings keep stakeholders updated.
- Information is kept visible and shared in real time, rather than issued in periodic formal reports.
- A flat organization tends to use open, informal channels; a hierarchical one uses more formal communication.

## Exam tips

- "Which document lists the stakeholders and their classification?" The **stakeholder register**. "Which says how to engage them?" The **stakeholder engagement plan**.
- A newly discovered stakeholder: **update the stakeholder register** first, then analyze and plan engagement. Never ignore them.
- High power, low interest: **keep satisfied**. Low power, high interest: **keep informed**.
- **C** and **D** on a matrix means the **stakeholder engagement assessment matrix**.
- Email and reports are **push**. An intranet site is **pull**. A meeting or call is **interactive**.
- For channels, **add the project manager** to the team count before using n(n − 1) ÷ 2.
- Acknowledging a message does **not** mean agreeing with it.
- A stakeholder who resists the project is engaged **more**, not avoided. Find out their concerns.
- Complex or sensitive matters are best handled **face to face** (interactive).
