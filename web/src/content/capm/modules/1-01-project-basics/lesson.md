## Key points

- A **project** is a temporary initiative in a unique context, undertaken to **create value**. **Operations** are ongoing and repeat.
- **Project management** applies knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to **meet or exceed the intended value**.
- A **program** groups *related* projects to obtain benefits not available from managing them one by one.
- A **portfolio** groups programs, projects and operations (related or not) to maximize value and achieve *strategic objectives*.
- The value chain: deliverable (output) → outcome → benefit (or disbenefit) → value.
- **Project success** has two dimensions: success of the *outcomes* and success of the *project management*.

## What is a project?

A project is a **temporary initiative in a unique context undertaken to create value**.

**Temporary** means the work has a defined beginning and end. It does not mean short, and the deliverables usually live on long after the project closes. A project normally ends when one of these is true:

- its objectives have been achieved
- the sponsor, a governing body or the team decides the objectives will not or cannot be met
- resources (money, people, materials) run out or are no longer available
- the need no longer exists, because strategy, priorities or the environment changed
- it is terminated for another reason, such as a legal, regulatory or compliance issue

**Unique context** means the specific conditions that make one project different from another, even when they look similar: goals, scope, duration, location, technology, quality, cost, risks, resources and stakeholders. Two housing units built by the same contractor in the same district still differ in buyer, lender, customization and ground conditions. This is why every project needs a tailored approach.

**Creating value through change.** A project moves an organization from its **current state** to a desired **future state**, sometimes through a planned **transition state**. Reaching the future state is what delivers the value.

> **Edition note.** The 7th edition defined a project as "a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result." The 8th edition shifts the emphasis from the unique *product* to the unique *context* and to *value*. Recognize both wordings.

## Why projects get started

Leaders authorize projects to respond to factors such as:

- meeting regulatory, legal or social requirements
- satisfying stakeholder requests or needs
- implementing or changing business or technology strategy
- creating, improving or fixing products, processes or services

Every project should link back to the organization's strategic objectives and business value.

## What is project management?

Project management is the application of **knowledge, skills, tools and techniques** to project activities **to meet or exceed the intended value**.

"Exceed" does not mean giving extras. It does *not* endorse **gold plating** (adding features nobody asked for) or **scope creep** (uncontrolled growth of scope). It means making value-driven decisions so the final outcome really satisfies stakeholder needs.

Related terms:

- **Project manager:** the person assigned by the performing organization to lead the team responsible for achieving the project objectives.
- **Project team:** the individuals performing the work of the project.
- **Project management team:** the project team members directly involved in project management activities.
- **PMO (project management office):** an organizational entity that centralizes activities related to managing portfolios, programs and/or projects. What it does varies by organization.

## Programs

A program is a group of **related** projects and program activities managed in a **coordinated** manner to obtain benefits not available from managing them individually.

A program is *not* just a large project. It creates synergy by connecting resources and aligning its projects, and it drives significant organizational change.

> Example: a "new metro line" program contains projects for tunnelling, stations, trains and ticketing. Each alone is of little use; coordinated, they deliver a working line.

## Portfolios

A portfolio is a collection of programs, projects and operations managed as a group to **maximize overall value delivery** and achieve strategic objectives, meet mandatory obligations, or generate income.

Portfolio management means **selecting, prioritizing, managing and optimizing** the organization's programs and projects in line with its strategy. The components do not have to be related to each other. Portfolio management is the link between strategy and execution.

## Operations

Operations management is about the **efficient, ongoing production** of products and services: turning inputs (materials, energy, labor) into outputs. Ongoing operations are outside the scope of a project.

Projects and operations intersect at certain points, for example when developing or upgrading a product, when improving an operational process, at the end of a product's life, and at each closeout phase or iteration. At those points deliverables, people and knowledge are transferred. **Engaging the operations team early** in project planning helps the result survive and succeed after handover.

Portfolios, programs, projects and operations often share the same stakeholders and **compete for the same resources**, so their managers need to work together.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Project | Program | Portfolio | Operations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Temporary initiative in a unique context | Related projects managed together | Programs, projects and operations grouped for strategy | Ongoing production of products and services |
| Focus | Creating the intended value | Benefits from coordination and synergy | Selecting and prioritizing the right work | Efficiency and continuity |
| Duration | Defined start and end | Until its benefits are delivered | Continues as strategy evolves | No planned end |
| Components related? | n/a | Yes | Not necessarily | n/a |

## From deliverable to value

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Output / deliverable** | The product, result or service generated | A new mobile banking app |
| **Outcome** | The end result or consequence; can be positive or negative | Customers bank on their phones |
| **Benefit** | A gain realized from an outcome | Fewer branch visits, lower cost |
| **Disbenefit** | A negative consequence or loss from an outcome | Some older customers feel excluded |
| **Value** | The excess of financial and non-financial benefits over the investment | Higher profit and satisfaction than the app cost to build |

Two more terms: an **artifact** is a document or other item created to help manage the work and inform people (a plan, a report, a register). A **product** is something produced and quantifiable, tangible or intangible.

**Value depends on who is looking.** An organization may measure business value with metrics such as return on investment. A customer may see value as convenience. Governments and NGOs may prioritize societal impact. Business value can be **tangible** (money, market share, infrastructure, productivity) or **intangible** (reputation, brand, goodwill, knowledge, employee well-being). Because every project is an investment, its expected value should meet or exceed a target threshold.

## The value delivery system

Portfolios, programs, projects, products and operations together form the organization's **value delivery system**: the strategic business activities that build, sustain or advance the organization. It sits inside the internal environment (policies, governance, methods), which sits inside the external environment (economy, competition, laws).

Information flows both ways:

- **Down:** senior leadership gives strategy to portfolios; portfolios give desired outcomes, benefits and value to programs and projects; these hand deliverables with support and maintenance information to operations.
- **Up:** operations send back information for fixes and adjustments; programs and projects report performance and progress; portfolios report portfolio performance to senior leadership.

## Assessing project success

**Project success** is the shared view of beneficiaries, stakeholders and participants that the project delivered value worth the effort and expense. It has two dimensions:

1. **Success of the project outcomes** (effectiveness): did the project realize the intended value? This can show up during the project, right after it, or years later.
2. **Success of project management** (efficiency): did the project stay within its constraints of cost, scope, time and quality?

Both matter, and they can diverge. The Sydney Opera House ran ten years late and cost more than fourteen times its budget, a project management failure, yet its outcome far exceeded expectations. A Montreal overpass was built well, on plan, and demolished a year later because it did not fit the adjacent bridge redevelopment: good project management, failed outcome.

## Exam tips

- **Ongoing and repetitive** work is operations, however large.
- "**Related**, coordinated, for extra **benefits**" → program. "Selected and prioritized for **strategic objectives**" → portfolio.
- "On time and on budget" alone does not equal success. Look for the answer about **value and outcomes**.
- Giving the customer extras they did not request is gold plating, and PMI treats it as wrong, not as exceeding value.
- When an option says a project ends "when the budget is spent", check the full list: objectives met, objectives unreachable, resources gone, need gone, or terminated.
