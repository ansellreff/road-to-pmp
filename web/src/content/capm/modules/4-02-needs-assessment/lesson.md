## Key points

- **Needs assessment** works out **what the real problem or opportunity is** and **whether to invest** in solving it. It is mostly **pre-project** work.
- It has **seven processes**, in order: identify the problem or opportunity, assess the current state, determine the future state, determine viable options and recommend, facilitate the product roadmap, assemble the business case, support charter development.
- **Agree on the situation before discussing solutions.** The **situation statement** records it: the problem, its effect, and the impact.
- **Current state** is "as-is". **Future state** is "to-be". The difference is found by **gap analysis**.
- The **business case** justifies the investment. An approved business case is an input to the **charter**.
- The **sponsor** owns the business case and issues the charter. The business analyst **supports** both.
- In adaptive projects the same thinking happens, but lightly, in **iteration 0** and again as the work proceeds.

## What needs assessment is for

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.3.3

**Needs assessment** analyzes current business problems or opportunities to understand what is needed to reach the desired future state. Its results **guide investment decisions**: which initiatives to pursue, which to stop, and what the scope of a new one should be.

| # | Process | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Identify Problem or Opportunity** | Business need; situation statement |
| 2 | **Assess Current State** | Current state assessment |
| 3 | **Determine Future State** | Business goals and objectives; required capabilities and features |
| 4 | **Determine Viable Options and Provide Recommendation** | Feasibility study results; recommended solution option |
| 5 | **Facilitate Product Roadmap Development** | Product roadmap |
| 6 | **Assemble Business Case** | Business case; product scope |
| 7 | **Support Charter Development** | Charter; shared product information |

The first six belong to the **Defining and Aligning** process group. **Support Charter Development** is the only process in the **Initiating** process group.

How formal this work is depends on the organization, its culture, the market and any regulation.

## Identify Problem or Opportunity

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.1.3.2; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Understand the Situation

The process of identifying the **problem to be solved** or the **opportunity to be pursued**. If the situation is not properly understood, the organization may pay for a solution that **does not address the business need**.

| Output | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Business need** | The **impetus for change**, based on an existing problem or opportunity. It is the reason a new project is being considered. |
| **Situation statement** | An **objective statement** of the problem or opportunity, with its **effect** on the organization and the resulting **impact** |

**Situation statement, in three parts:**

| Part | Example |
|---|---|
| **The problem or opportunity** | Customers wait an average of 12 minutes to reach the support desk |
| **Its effect** | One caller in four hangs up before being answered |
| **The impact** | Complaints have doubled and renewals fell by 8% this year |

- The format matters less than the result: **the team agrees on the situation before talking about solutions**.
- **Measure the size** of the problem with data, so the solution is neither too small nor too large. Where the organization has no data, use **benchmarking**.

Techniques used: **benchmarking** (comparing with standards or "best in class" organizations), **competitive analysis**, **market analysis**, **document analysis**, **interviews** and **prototyping**.

## Assess Current State

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.2; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.2.3.1

The process of examining the current environment to understand the internal and external factors that may be the **cause** of the problem or opportunity. This is the **as-is** analysis.

- The output is the **current state assessment**: an understanding of how the organization operates today.
- A main focus is **capabilities**. A **capability** is a function, process, service or other proficiency of the organization.
- Learn **enough** to understand the situation. A full requirements analysis is **not** needed yet.
- It need not be a formal document. Existing enterprise and business architecture may be enough.
- It shows what will **stay unchanged** and what **must change**.

## Finding root causes

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.2.2.9; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.2.2.10; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.2.2.7

A problem is **analyzed before it is acted on**. A problem is broken down into its **root causes**; an opportunity into its **contributors**.

| Technique | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Five whys** | Keep asking why the problem occurs until the root cause is clear, typically within five rounds. It is a line of deeper questioning, not literally the word "why" five times. |
| **Fishbone diagram** (cause-and-effect, Ishikawa) | The **problem at the head**, causes and sub-causes on the bones, grouped by category. A good starting point. |
| **Interrelationship diagram** | Shows how causes and effects **relate to each other**, where the cause of one problem is the effect of another. Finds the most significant causes. |
| **Pareto diagram** | A bar chart in **descending order**, showing the few causes responsible for most of the problem (the 80/20 principle) |
| **Process flow** | Shows how the work is done today. A **value stream map** separates steps that add value from **waste**. |
| **Capability table** | Lists each problem, its root causes, and the capability needed to address it |

@diagram fishbone

**SWOT analysis** looks at a situation from four sides:

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** | **Strengths** | **Weaknesses** |
| **External** | **Opportunities** | **Threats** |

Strengths and weaknesses belong to the **organization**. Opportunities and threats come from the **outside environment**.

## Determine Future State

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.3; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.3.2.6; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.3.3.2

The process of determining the **gaps in existing capabilities** and the changes needed to reach a desired future state. This is the **to-be** analysis.

| Output | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Business goals and objectives** | What the business expects the project to deliver, as **stated targets** |
| **Required capabilities and features** | The **net changes** the organization needs. They **do not prescribe a solution**. |

**Gap analysis** compares two things, usually the as-is and the to-be:

> **Required capabilities − existing capabilities = the gap**

The gap is the set of **missing capabilities** the organization must acquire.

Other techniques:

| Technique | Use |
|---|---|
| **Affinity diagram** | Groups many ideas, for example from brainstorming, into related sets |
| **Feature model** | Shows the features of a solution as a tree; one version per future-state alternative |
| **Kano analysis** | Classifies features by their effect on customer satisfaction |
| **Purpose alignment model** | Sorts features by how **mission critical** and how **market differentiating** they are |
| **Solution capability matrix** | Shows which capability is covered by which component of the solution |

**Purpose alignment model:**

| Category | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Differentiating** | Mission critical and differentiating | Invest and excel |
| **Parity** | Mission critical, not differentiating | Keep up with competitors |
| **Partner** | Differentiating, not mission critical | Find a partner to provide it |
| **Who cares** | Neither | Generally not built |

## Determine Viable Options and Provide Recommendation

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.4.3.2

The process of examining possible solutions and deciding which is the **best for the organization to pursue**. Stakeholders often propose a solution without understanding the situation; presenting this analysis makes an unfit choice less likely.

**The activities:**

1. **Identify viable options**
2. **Conduct feasibility analysis** on each
3. **Define preliminary product scope**: the high-level capabilities each option provides
4. **Define high-level transition requirements**, such as data conversion and training
5. **Recommend the most viable option**, with the reason it was chosen over the others

**Feasibility factors:**

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| **Operational feasibility** | Will the organization accept the change and sustain it? |
| **Technology feasibility** | Do the technology and skills exist, or can they be obtained affordably? Does it fit existing systems? |
| **Cost-effectiveness feasibility** | A first, high-level estimate of costs against benefits |
| **Time feasibility** | Can it be delivered within the time constraints? |
| **Supportability** | How easily can it be maintained over time? |
| **Value** | What business value will it provide, and how may that change over time? |
| Also | Constraints, assumptions, product risks, dependencies, culture, validation |

No single factor decides feasibility. Several are weighed together.

**What to recommend:**

- **One** option is feasible: usually recommend it.
- **Several** are feasible: **rank them**, for example with weighted ranking.
- **None** is viable: recommending that **nothing be done** is a valid outcome.

**Weighted ranking.** Give each criterion a weight, rate each option against each criterion, multiply, and add.

| | Cost (weight 0.4) | Speed (weight 0.6) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Option A** | 3 × 0.4 = 1.2 | 1 × 0.6 = 0.6 | **1.8** |
| **Option B** | 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 | 3 × 0.6 = 1.8 | **2.6** |

Option B is preferred. The weights must reflect the business goals and objectives.

Other techniques: **cost-benefit analysis** (benefits against costs, often with help from a financial analyst), **valuation techniques** (payback period, ROI, IRR, NPV), **benchmarking**, **group decision-making techniques**, and **real options** (make fewer decisions now and delay the rest as late as possible).

## Facilitate Product Roadmap Development

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.5; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.5.3.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.5.2.3

A **product roadmap** gives a **high-level view of the product's features and the sequence** in which they will be built and delivered. It shows how the product will develop over time.

- Key benefit: **shared expectations** among stakeholders about what will be delivered and in what order.
- It links **organizational strategy** and the **product vision** to a plan that can be carried out.
- It is built **collaboratively** by business and development people.
- The business analyst **facilitates**; the roadmap is not the analyst's alone.

What a roadmap usually holds: strategy information, the **product vision**, **product releases** with their themes or high-level features, **features** paired to releases, success criteria, milestones and market forces.

| Technique | Use |
|---|---|
| **Product visioning** | Sets the high-level direction. Produces a **vision statement**: target market, users, main benefits, and what sets the product apart. |
| **Story mapping** | Sequences user stories by business value and the order users perform them. Stories at this stage are high-level and may be **epics**. |
| **Feature model** | Supplies the list of features |
| **Facilitated workshop** | A structured meeting led by a skilled, **neutral facilitator** with selected stakeholders |

**Which features go into which release?** Features are prioritized by how each supports the strategy and business objectives, then allocated to releases in that order, allowing for dependencies.

## Which features go into which release

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 7.2.2.16; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 8.3.2.11; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.5.3.1

A roadmap pairs **features with releases**. A **story map** is the usual tool for deciding the split.

| Part of a story map | What it holds | Goes into |
|---|---|---|
| **Backbone** | The **minimum set of capabilities** the solution must have to serve its purpose. Often called the minimum viable product (MVP). | The **first release** |
| **Walking skeleton** | The **full end-to-end functionality** stakeholders need to accept the solution. Sometimes called the minimum marketable features. | The early releases |
| **Stories below** | Further stories under each capability, **highest value at the top** | Later releases, from the top down |

Rules for allocating features to releases:

1. **Value first:** the highest-value items go earliest.
2. **Respect dependencies:** an item cannot ship before what it relies on.
3. **Each release must be usable** on its own, end to end.
4. **Capacity limits the size** of a release.
5. The allocation is **revisited** as priorities change.

## Assemble Business Case

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.6; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.6.3.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.6.3.2

A **business case** is a **documented economic feasibility study**. It establishes whether the benefits justify the investment, and gives decision makers what they need to decide whether the initiative is **worth pursuing**.

**Minimum contents:**

| Part | Holds |
|---|---|
| **Problem or opportunity** | The situation statement, supporting data, the stakeholders affected |
| **Analysis of the situation** | Root causes or contributors; needed versus existing capabilities; how a solution supports the business goals |
| **Recommendation** | Feasibility results for each option; constraints, assumptions, risks and dependencies; the ranked alternatives; the recommended option and why; the cost-benefit summary; the implementation approach |
| **Evaluation** | How the benefits will be measured |

- It is the **link** between business goals and the projects set up to achieve them.
- An **approved** business case is an **input to the charter**.
- Decision makers may **accept** the recommendation, **choose another option**, **defer** or **reject**. The approved option defines the **initial product scope**.
- **Not every initiative needs a formal business case.** Some are approved because of competitive pressure, a government mandate or executive decision. The thinking still applies.
- The **sponsor** owns the business case: the sponsor writes it with the analyst's support, or supplies the information for it, and seeks its approval.

**Product scope** is the features and functions that characterize a solution. At this point it is known only at a high level, and it keeps being refined.

## Support Charter Development

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.7; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.7.3.1

A **charter** formally **authorizes** the project and gives the project manager authority to use organizational resources. It sets the **scope boundaries** and records the start of the initiative.

- It is **issued by the sponsor** or initiator, who must be senior enough to obtain funding and commit resources.
- The business analyst **supports** its development with the knowledge gained in needs assessment. The analyst does **not** issue it.
- In a predictive life cycle, the analyst supports the sponsor. In an adaptive one, a **product owner** may contribute the business analysis.
- It is developed **collaboratively**, which gives the team a **shared understanding** of the solution.
- Its information is high-level, but **more detailed** than the recommendation, and more **tactical**: how the initiative will be carried out.

Typical contents: description and purpose, business goals and objectives, high-level product and project scope, risks, a summary milestone schedule, summary budget, and key stakeholders.

| | Business case | Charter |
|---|---|---|
| **Question answered** | **Should** we do this? | We are doing this. **What** is authorized? |
| **Nature** | Economic justification | Formal authorization |
| **Comes** | First | After the business case is approved |
| **Owner** | Sponsor, supported by the analyst | Sponsor, supported by the analyst and project manager |

## Predictive and adaptive needs assessment

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.1.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.4.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 4.6.4

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | Before initiation, as a formal process | Before initiation or in **iteration 0**; revisited throughout |
| **Problem statement** | A situation statement that is drafted, reviewed and approved | A brief **statement of intent**, perhaps with high-level epics |
| **Current and future state** | Modeled in detail up front | **Just enough** to move forward, explored in slices |
| **Options** | Feasibility study completed and option chosen before the project | Options tried out with **spikes** during iterations |
| **Business case** | Detailed, formally signed off; funding for the whole development | **Lightweight**, revisited and revised |
| **Return on investment** | At the end of the initiative | **Sooner**, as features are delivered iteratively |
| **Feasibility proven** | Late, so the risk is higher | **Early**, by delivering higher-risk features first |

## The practice guide on assessing business value

@source Business Analysis for Practitioners, Value of Understanding the Situation; Business Analysis for Practitioners, How to Understand the Situation; Business Analysis for Practitioners, How to Find the Gaps; Business Analysis for Practitioners, How to Define the Solution; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Glossary; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Figure 7-6

*Business Analysis for Practitioners* calls needs assessment the **Business Value Assessment** domain and divides it into three practices. They match the processes above, with details of their own that the exam can ask for.

**Understand the Situation**

- **Start with why.** The guide uses the **Golden Circle**: why, then how, then what. The analyst finds the business need (the why) **before** the outcome (the what) and the way to reach it (the how). This is what stops a team from rushing to a solution.
- Six things are elicited: **signs** of a problem or opportunity, the **related stakeholders**, the **severity or magnitude**, a **situation statement**, the **root causes**, and the **goals and objectives** it relates to.
- **Not every problem deserves action.** Without measuring the magnitude, a proposed solution may be too small or too large for the situation. A 5% staff turnover may be serious among knowledge workers and unremarkable elsewhere.
- A **situation statement** has three parts: the **problem or opportunity**; its **direct effect**, usually the most noticeable part; and the **business impact**, what it costs or could return. The format matters less than the agreement: stakeholders agree on the statement **before** solutions are explored. In the session, the group is asked to stay on the problem and not offer solutions, and the work ends when consensus is reached.
- Objectives should be **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. (The guide notes variants: attainable or assignable for A, realistic or reasonable for R.)
- Root cause techniques it lists: fishbone diagrams, five whys, interrelationship diagrams, Pareto charts, and process analysis, including **SIPOC** (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers) and value stream maps.

**Find the Gaps**

- The gaps are in **capabilities** (people, processes, technology, products) and in **performance indicators**, such as KPIs or **objectives and key results (OKRs)**.
- Internal and external factors can be examined with **PESTLE** (political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental) or **CATWOE** (customer, actor, transformation, worldview, owner, environment).
- The result is a list of **net changes** the organization must make. That list is **not yet a solution**: how the capabilities will be delivered is decided next.

**Define the Solution**

| Step | What is done |
|---|---|
| 1 | Determine the options and recommend |
| 2 | For each option, identify assumptions, constraints, risks, dependencies, benefits and **disbenefits** |
| 3 | Evaluate feasibility and viability; discard the infeasible |
| 4 | Assemble the business case, a full one **only for the most viable options** |
| 5 | Determine the solution approach, with a roadmap |

- **What to recommend:** one feasible option, recommend it; several, rank them (multicriteria decision analysis, also called weighted ranking); **none, recommend that nothing be done**.
- The **decision package** may hold a business case, a product roadmap, the high-level product scope and a **benefits realization plan**.
- A business case has at least four parts: **problem or opportunity**, **analysis of the situation**, **recommendation** and **evaluation** (how benefits will be measured). It is a **living document**, reviewed and updated as the project goes on.
- **Disbenefits** are identified, quantified and measured in the same way as benefits.
- A roadmap lets the analyst **defer** a stakeholder's need to a later point instead of rejecting it, which lowers resistance.

Two more tools from its glossary: a **customer journey map** shows the phases customers go through when using a product and what they experience at each point; an **empathy map** records what one type of user **says, thinks, does and feels**.

> **On the exam.** A **capability table** is used **after root cause analysis**. Its columns run from the **problem**, to its **root causes**, to the **current capabilities**, to the **new capabilities required**, and last to the **deliverables that fill the gap**. It lets everyone see that each part of the solution answers a root cause.

## Tools in the practice guide: finding the cause and judging options

@source Business Analysis for Practitioners, Figure 7-8; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Figure 7-7; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Figure 7-10; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Figure 7-17; Business Analysis for Practitioners, Figure 7-11

*Business Analysis for Practitioners* describes these tools for assessing a need. The outline asks you to match a tool to a situation.

| Tool | What it is | Points the guide makes |
|---|---|---|
| **Five whys** | Finding possible root causes by asking "why" again and again | It need **not be exactly five** times. Often used **with a fishbone diagram**, because a problem usually has many root causes. **Human error and inattention are not root causes.** Assess the problem, not the people. Different teams may reach different causes. |
| **Feasibility analysis** | An in-depth look at proposed solution options from several angles, to see that they are viable | An initial risk assessment that helps decide **whether to proceed**. Elements include **operational**, **technology**, **time** and **cost-effectiveness** feasibility, with constraints, assumptions, product risks, dependencies, culture, value and validation. |
| **Force field analysis** | Listing the **driving forces** that support a change and the **restraining forces** that resist it | Each force is given a weight. The totals are compared to see the net acceptance of the change. The action plan **strengthens the driving forces and weakens the restraining ones**. |
| **Real options** | A way of thinking about **when** to decide, not how or why | Keep options open as long as possible, gather information meanwhile, and commit only at the **last responsible moment**, when you must or have good reason to. |
| **Key performance indicators (KPIs)** | Metrics chosen as key indicators of progress toward business objectives | **Not every metric is a KPI.** A KPI is objective, comparable with earlier results and tied to the business value stream. KPIs are **not goals**, and should not change so often that progress cannot be tracked. |

## Exam tips

- A stakeholder arrives with a solution already chosen: first **understand the problem**. Ask what need it is meant to meet.
- "As-is" means **current state**. "To-be" means **future state**. The difference is the **gap**.
- **Strengths and weaknesses are internal. Opportunities and threats are external.**
- "Ask why repeatedly" is **five whys**. "Head and bones" is the **fishbone** diagram.
- The question "is it **worth** doing?" is answered by the **business case**.
- The document that **authorizes** the project is the **charter**, issued by the **sponsor**.
- Order: situation statement, current state, future state, options, roadmap, business case, charter.
- Required capabilities **do not prescribe a solution**. Requirements should stay independent of design.
- If no option is viable, **doing nothing** can be the recommendation.
- A product roadmap shows **what** will be delivered and in **what sequence**, at a high level. It is not a detailed schedule.
