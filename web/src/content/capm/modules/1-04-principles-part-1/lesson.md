## Key points

- The principles are **guidance, not rules**. They shape the mindset and behavior of people on projects, and they never contradict each other.
- There are **six principles**, grouped under a mindset with **three dimensions**: proactive, ownership and value-driven.
- Principles are the **mindset**. Performance domains are the **mechanics**. Domains put principles into practice.
- **Adopt a Holistic View:** treat the project as a system of connected parts (systems thinking).
- **Focus on Value:** value is the ultimate indicator of success. Aim at outcomes, not just deliverables.
- **Embed Quality Into Processes and Deliverables:** build quality in from the start through prevention, continuous improvement and waste elimination.

## What a principle is

In some professions principles are laws. In project management they are **not prescriptive**. They are foundational guidelines for strategy, decision-making and problem-solving, meant to reinforce the right mindset. The PMBOK standard is therefore called a **principle-based** standard.

How a principle is applied depends on context: the organization, the project, the deliverables, the team and the stakeholders. The principles **complement** each other and may overlap, but no principle contradicts another.

## The six principles and the mindset

| Mindset dimension | What it emphasizes | Principles |
|---|---|---|
| **Proactive** | Systems thinking, the right level of planning, anticipating problems, continuous improvement | Adopt a Holistic View; Embed Quality Into Processes and Deliverables |
| **Ownership** | Accountability of leaders and a high-performance, self-reliant team culture | Be an Accountable Leader; Build an Empowered Culture |
| **Value-driven** | Maximum value, with sustainability built in through the whole life cycle | Focus on Value; Integrate Sustainability Within All Project Areas |

The project management mindset is sometimes called a **growth mindset**. The value-driven dimension links to the **triple bottom line: people, profit and planet**.

> **Edition note.** The 7th edition had **12 principles** (stewardship, team, stakeholders, value, systems thinking, leadership, tailoring, quality, complexity, risk, adaptability and resiliency, change). The 8th edition consolidates them into **six**. Module 1.11 maps the old list to the new one.

## Principles and performance domains

- **Principles = mindset.** They guide how you think and behave.
- **Performance domains = mechanics.** They contain the knowledge, processes and methods used to deliver.

The seven performance domains are **Governance, Scope (including quality), Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders, Resources and Risk**. Each principle relates to all domains, some more strongly than others:

| Principle | Most strongly connected domains |
|---|---|
| Adopt a Holistic View | All seven |
| Focus on Value; Embed Quality | Governance, Scope, Risk, Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders |
| Be an Accountable Leader | Governance, Stakeholders, Risk |
| Integrate Sustainability; Build an Empowered Culture | All seven |

## Principle 1: Adopt a Holistic View

Understand and manage the project by considering **all its components and their interdependencies as part of a larger system**. This is **systems thinking**: seeing relationships and patterns, not isolated snapshots.

Why it matters: when you see the whole, you can trace problems to their **root causes**, manage complexity, and avoid fixing one part while breaking another.

A project managed holistically shows:

- **alignment with organizational strategy** and goals
- **integrated decision-making** that considers perspectives across the whole project
- **enhanced communication** across all stakeholders
- **better handling of uncertainty and complexity**, by progressively identifying sources of uncertainty and simplifying interdependencies
- **proactive, pragmatic risk management**: mitigating threats and exploiting opportunities early

> Example from the book: an NGO runs a public health campaign by producing materials and events. Midway, it learns the local government has a similar initiative with grant funding. By looking beyond its own plan and aligning with that initiative, it gains resources and reach, at the price of stricter rules.

## Principle 2: Focus on Value

**Value is the ultimate success indicator and driver of projects.** It is the overall worth of the outcomes and the net benefit to stakeholders. It can be measured (return on investment) or described qualitatively (testimonials, societal benefit).

What this principle asks of the team:

- Use the **business case** (project justification and strategy) to make decisions that meet or exceed the intended business value.
- Describe desired outcomes clearly, then **assess and update them throughout the project** with quality gates, feedback loops and regular reviews.
- **If the project is unlikely to deliver the intended value, it may be best to terminate it.**
- Maximize return by delivering the required functionality and quality with **minimal necessary resources**, avoiding rework and other waste.
- In adaptive projects without fixed scope, work with the customer to decide which features are worth the time and money.

Value can be realized **during the project, at the end, or after completion**. The payback period may extend well beyond project closeout.

**Outcomes over deliverables.** A deliverable can be finished and still not produce the intended outcome. The book's example: a customer wants new software to raise productivity. The software is the output, but it does not create productivity on its own. Adding **training** as a deliverable is what enables the outcome.

> Second example: when rolling out an internal system, the conventional choice is the product with the most features. A value-focused team notices the company culture prefers simplicity, so it chooses fewer features and less customization, and gets higher adoption.

In practice this principle keeps governance "right-sized" (value, not bureaucracy) and guards scope against **scope creep and gold plating**.

## Principle 3: Embed Quality Into Processes and Deliverables

**Quality** is the degree to which the inherent characteristics of a deliverable or process help meet or exceed the project's target objectives. It applies to **both** what you produce and how you produce it.

Quality is measured by conformance to **acceptance criteria**, the **definition of done (DoD)**, **fitness for use**, and overall efficiency. The foundations are **continuous improvement** and **waste elimination**.

Dimensions of quality:

| Dimension | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Performance | Does it function as intended? |
| Conformity | Does it match specifications and is it fit for use? |
| Reliability | Is it consistent? |
| Resilience | Can it cope with failures and recover quickly? |
| Satisfaction | Do customers and users respond well (usability, experience)? |
| Uniformity | Is it on par with similar outputs? |
| Efficiency | Greatest output for least input? |
| Sustainability | Positive economic, social and environmental effect? |
| Compliance | Does it meet regulations and standards? |

How teams pursue quality:

- for the **product**: training, inspections and testing
- for the **process**: reviews and audits

Both aim to **prevent** errors and catch defects early. The results are less rework and scrap, fewer complaints, timely delivery, better cost control, higher team morale, and less environmental impact.

A related idea is **shift-left**: put quality practices as early in the life cycle as possible. Quality is most closely tied to the **Scope** performance domain, which in the 8th edition includes quality.

> Example: a shipping company entering a new market could simply meet the government's shipping regulations. A quality-driven approach also investigates distributors and retailers, and finds that high-value customers have stricter standards than the regulator. Meeting those leads to a stronger market entry.

## Exam tips

- A question that asks what a principle *is*: guidance for behavior and mindset, not a mandatory rule or process.
- "Considers interdependencies", "sees the project as a system", "root cause": **Adopt a Holistic View**.
- "Outcome rather than output", "business case", "terminate if the value is no longer there": **Focus on Value**.
- "Prevention", "built in", "acceptance criteria", "continuous improvement": **Embed Quality**.
- PMI prefers **prevention over inspection**. Finding defects late is always the more expensive answer.
- Know the pairs: proactive (holistic, quality), ownership (leader, culture), value-driven (value, sustainability).
