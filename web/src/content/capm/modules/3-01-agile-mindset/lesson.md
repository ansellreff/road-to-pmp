## Key points

- **Agile is a mindset**, defined by **4 values** and guided by **12 principles**, and carried out through many different practices.
- Agile suits **high-uncertainty work**: new designs, changing requirements, problems that must be explored.
- The four values: **individuals and interactions**, **working product**, **customer collaboration**, **responding to change**.
- **Iterative** = improve the work through repeated cycles of feedback. **Incremental** = deliver finished pieces the customer can use. **Agile does both.**
- Agile delivers value **early and often**, in short cycles, with the **highest-value work first**.
- Every approach plans. The difference is **how much and when**.
- **Lean** is the wider family. **Agile** and **Kanban** are both descended from lean thinking.

## Definable work and high-uncertainty work

@source Agile Practice Guide, 2.1

| | Definable work | High-uncertainty work (knowledge work) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Clear procedures that have worked on similar projects before | New design and problem-solving |
| Examples | Building a car, an appliance or a house once the design is complete | Software, product design, research, AI and biotechnology projects |
| Uncertainty and risk | Low | High rates of change, complexity and risk |
| Best approach | **Predictive**: define the requirements up front and control change | **Agile**: explore in short cycles and adapt from feedback |

Predictive approaches struggle with high-uncertainty work because they try to fix most requirements at the start and treat change as an exception. Agile approaches were created to **test feasibility in short cycles** and **adapt quickly**, so that the team does not invest in the wrong solution.

## The Agile Manifesto: four values

@source Agile Practice Guide, 2.2

In **2001**, a group of software practitioners published the **Manifesto for Agile Software Development**. It states four values:

| We value more | Over |
|---|---|
| **Individuals and interactions** | Processes and tools |
| **Working software** | Comprehensive documentation |
| **Customer collaboration** | Contract negotiation |
| **Responding to change** | Following a plan |

- The items on the right **still have value**. The items on the left are valued **more**. Agile does not mean no documentation, no plan or no contract.
- Outside software, "working software" is often read as "working **solutions**" or "working product".
- The values began in software and have spread to fields such as healthcare, education and engineering.

## The twelve principles in plain words

@source Agile Practice Guide, 2.2

| # | Principle | Short form |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of something valuable. This is the **highest priority**. | Deliver value early |
| 2 | Welcome changing requirements, even late. Change is a competitive advantage for the customer. | Welcome change |
| 3 | Deliver working results frequently, every few weeks to a couple of months; shorter is better. | Deliver often |
| 4 | Business people and the team work together **daily**. | Collaborate daily |
| 5 | Build projects around motivated people; give them support and **trust** them. | Trust motivated people |
| 6 | **Face-to-face** conversation is the most efficient way to share information. | Talk face to face |
| 7 | A **working product** is the primary measure of progress. | Working product = progress |
| 8 | Work at a **sustainable pace** that can be kept up indefinitely. | Sustainable pace |
| 9 | Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design. | Technical excellence |
| 10 | **Simplicity**: maximize the amount of work **not** done. | Keep it simple |
| 11 | The best designs and requirements emerge from **self-organizing teams**. | Self-organizing teams |
| 12 | The team regularly **reflects** on how to become more effective and adjusts. | Reflect and adjust |

Principle 12 is the basis for the **retrospective**. Principle 7 is why agile teams report progress by what is **finished and working**, not by percent complete.

## Mindset, values, principles, practices

@source Agile Practice Guide, 2.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.1

Agile is a **mindset**, **defined by** the four values, **guided by** the twelve principles, and **shown through** many practices. Teams choose the practices that fit their needs.

- The agile mindset is about **being agile**, not just **doing agile**. A team can hold daily meetings and use a task board and still not be agile if it does not deliver value early, welcome change and learn from feedback.
- "Agile" is an **umbrella term** for many frameworks: Scrum, Extreme Programming (XP), Kanban, Crystal, Feature-Driven Development (FDD), the Dynamic Systems Development Method (DSDM).

Questions an agile team keeps asking:

- What can we **deliver quickly** to get early feedback?
- How are we **learning and adapting** from real results?
- How can we work **transparently**?
- What work can be **avoided or delayed** so we focus on what matters most?

Two ways to adopt agile: take up an established framework and learn it properly **before tailoring it**, or change current practices step by step toward the agile values. Tailoring too early can remove the benefit.

## Lean, agile and Kanban

@source Agile Practice Guide, 2.3

**Lean thinking** is the wider family. **Agile** and the **Kanban Method** are both descended from it and share its ideas:

- **focus on value**,
- **small batch sizes**,
- **elimination of waste**,
- respect for people, transparency and continuous improvement.

The **Kanban Method** came from lean manufacturing and was adapted for knowledge work in the mid-2000s. It is **less prescriptive** than other approaches and is known as the "**start where you are**" approach, because a team can apply it to its existing way of working.

Two related approaches help decide **what** to build before building it:

| Approach | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Design thinking** | Understanding user needs through observation and empathy |
| **Lean Startup** | Quickly building and testing a **minimum viable product (MVP)** to check assumptions |

## Iterative, incremental and agile

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.9; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 3.1.2; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 3.1.3; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, Table 3-1

Approaches sit on a **continuum** from predictive to agile, with iterative and incremental approaches in between.

| Approach | Requirements | Activities | Delivery | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Predictive** | Fixed up front; change is controlled | Done once for the whole project | Single or phased delivery | Manage cost and time and meet objectives |
| **Iterative** | Can evolve | **Repeated** until correct | Often a single delivery | **Correctness** of the solution, through feedback on unfinished work |
| **Incremental** | Can evolve | Done once per increment | **Frequent smaller deliveries** | **Speed**: finished pieces the customer can use now |
| **Agile** | Dynamic | Repeated until correct | **Frequent small deliveries** | **Customer value** through frequent delivery and feedback |

- **Iterations** allow feedback on partly finished work, to improve it.
- **Increments** provide finished deliverables the customer may be able to use immediately.
- **Agile combines both**: the team iterates over the product to create finished increments.
- Because the highest-value work is delivered first, agile can give an **earlier return on investment**.
- A predictive project typically delivers business value only **at the end**.
- "Hybrid" simply means a mix. It says little unless you describe which parts are predictive and which are agile.

> **On the exam.** Table 3-1 of the 2017 guide gives each life cycle a few words per column, and questions quote them. **Predictive:** requirements **fixed**, activities performed once for the entire project, a **single delivery**, goal **manage cost**. **Iterative:** requirements dynamic, activities repeated until correct, a **single delivery**, goal **correctness of the solution**. **Incremental:** requirements dynamic, activities performed once for a given increment, **frequent smaller deliveries**, goal **speed**. **Agile:** requirements dynamic, activities repeated until correct, **frequent small deliveries**, goal **customer value** through frequent deliveries and feedback. An iterative life cycle may take **longer**, because it is optimized for learning, not for speed of delivery.

## How an agile life cycle runs

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.9.2; Agile Practice Guide, 3.9.1

1. Requirements are kept in a **prioritized list** (the product backlog).
2. The team works in **iterations**, usually **1 to 4 weeks** long.
3. For each iteration, the **team itself** decides how much of the highest-priority work it can complete, and estimates it.
4. At the end of each iteration the team **demonstrates** what it finished.
5. Feedback changes the plan for the next iteration.

Kanban is the exception: it uses a **continuous pull** of work without iterations, but still aims for frequent delivery.

**Planning is always there.** What differs is how much is done and when:

| | Planning |
|---|---|
| **Predictive** | As much as possible up front. **The plan drives the work.** |
| **Iterative / incremental** | Up front, then adjusted by reviews of early work or by each delivery |
| **Agile** | A little at a time: **plan, deliver, learn, replan**, throughout the project |

## What changes when a team works in an agile way

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.9; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2, Figure 4-3

| | Predictive | Agile |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Fixed early | Expected to change |
| **What is fixed** | **Scope**; time and cost are estimated to fit | **Time and cost**; scope is adjusted to fit (the "inverted triangle") |
| Change | Controlled through change requests | Welcomed; handled through the backlog |
| Delivery of value | At the end | Early and often |
| Customer involvement | Mostly at the start and at acceptance | Continuous |
| Progress measured by | Performance against the plan | Finished, working product |
| Team | Directed by the project manager | Self-organizing, supported by a servant leader |

**Benefits commonly associated with agile:** earlier return on investment, lower risk of building the wrong thing, visibility for the customer, faster response to change, and a more engaged team.

Agile is not always the right choice. For defined, repeatable work with little uncertainty, a predictive "**plan the work, work the plan**" approach is efficient and appropriate.

## Pros and cons of adaptive and predictive approaches

@source Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 2.4; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 3.1

The exam outline asks you to compare the two. Neither is better in general: each fits a kind of work.

| | Predictive (plan-based) | Adaptive (agile) |
|---|---|---|
| **Works best when** | Requirements are clear and stable; the work has been done before | Requirements are uncertain or changing; the work is new |
| **Advantages** | Clear plan, budget and dates up front; easy to coordinate many parties; suits contracts and regulation | Early and frequent delivery; fast feedback; change is cheap; risk is found early |
| **Disadvantages** | Change is costly and slow; value arrives at the end; a wrong requirement is found late | Final scope, cost and date are less certain at the start; needs an available customer and a dedicated team |
| **Main risk** | Building the wrong thing well | Never converging, or delivering pieces that do not add up |

As uncertainty rises, so do **change, wasted work and rework**. Short cycles limit how much can be wasted before the team learns.

## What helps or hinders agile in an organization

@source Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 6.1.2; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 6.2; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 6.7

The outline asks which **enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)** and **organizational process assets (OPAs)** make adaptive work easier. EEFs are conditions the team does not control, such as culture and structure. OPAs are the organization's own policies, processes and knowledge.

| | Helps adaptive work | Hinders it |
|---|---|---|
| **Culture** (EEF) | A **safe** environment where people can be honest and experiment; willingness of executives to change | Blame; a habit of detailed long-range plans that must not change |
| **Structure** (EEF) | **Cross-functional** teams; people **dedicated** to one project | **Departmental silos**; a highly **functional** structure that resists collaboration across departments |
| **Geography** (EEF) | Colocated teams, or good tools for distributed ones | Dispersed teams with competing regional goals, language barriers, low visibility |
| **People policies** (OPA) | Reviews and rewards for **team** results; staff encouraged to broaden their skills | Leaders rewarded for local efficiency; narrow specialists with no incentive to diversify |
| **Funding and metrics** (OPA) | Short-term budgeting and measures that follow delivered value | Annual fixed budgets tied to a fixed scope |
| **Portfolio practice** (OPA) | Few projects at a time, finished one after another | People pulled onto **too many projects at once** |
| **Procurement** (OPA) | Long-term partnerships with suppliers who can work incrementally | Contracts won on short-term price; knowledge leaves with the vendor |

**Which structure suits adaptive work?** A **project-oriented** or strong matrix structure with dedicated, cross-functional, preferably colocated teams. A **functional** or weak matrix structure makes it hard, because team members answer to department managers and work on several things at once.

Where the organization is not ready, introduce agile practices **gradually**, one project at a time, with visible executive sponsorship.

## Agile contracts, the agile PMO and change

@source Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 6.3; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 6.6; Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, 6.1.1

Two parts of the organization that the 2017 guide describes in detail are how suppliers are **contracted** and how the **PMO** behaves. Both can make adaptive work easier or harder.

**Contracts.** The Manifesto values customer collaboration over contract negotiation. A contract can share risk and reward so that both sides win:

| Technique | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Multi-tiered structure** | Fixed items (warranties, arbitration) sit in a **master agreement**; items that may change (rates, product descriptions) in a **schedule of services**; scope, schedule and budget in a lightweight **statement of work** |
| **Emphasize value delivered** | Milestones and payments follow **value-driven deliverables**, not phase gates or intermediate documents |
| **Fixed-price increments** | The scope is split into small fixed-price pieces, such as user stories |
| **Not-to-exceed time and materials** | The total budget is **capped**; a new idea replaces original work of the same size |
| **Graduated time and materials** | A **higher** hourly rate for early delivery, a **lower** one for late delivery |
| **Early cancellation option** | If enough value has been delivered, the customer may stop and pay a **cancellation fee** instead of buying the rest |
| **Dynamic scope option** | With a fixed budget, the customer may **vary the scope** at set points |
| **Team augmentation** | The supplier's people join the customer's team; the **team** is funded, not a scope. The most collaborative form. |
| **Favor full-service suppliers** | Prefer suppliers that deliver complete value over many suppliers that each do one piece |

**The agile PMO** has three characteristics:

| An agile PMO is | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Value-driven** | It works like a consultancy, giving each project what that project needs: templates for one, coaching for another |
| **Invitation-oriented** | It does not mandate. Those who are interested are invited to use its services, which raises engagement |
| **Multidisciplinary** | It has skills beyond project management, such as organizational design and change management |

Some PMOs become **agile centers of excellence**: they provide standards and templates, training and mentoring, coordination between agile teams, organizational learning (velocity profiles, retrospective findings), product owner training, help in recruiting team leaders, and specialists such as retrospective facilitators.

**Change management.** Two things make it matter more in agile work: **accelerated delivery** (the receiving organization must be able to absorb outputs at the faster pace, or the return is delayed) and the **change to agile approaches itself** (more handoffs, and rework that may be viewed negatively).

## Exam tips

- "Agile is…" a **mindset**. Not a methodology, not a tool, not a process.
- The right-hand items of the Manifesto **still have value**. An answer saying agile has "no documentation" or "no planning" is wrong.
- **Highest priority** in agile: **satisfy the customer** through early and continuous delivery of value.
- **Primary measure of progress**: a **working product**.
- Feedback on **unfinished** work = **iterative**. Delivering **finished** pieces = **incremental**.
- Changing requirements in an agile project are **welcome**. Do not choose "submit a change request to the change control board".
- **Kanban** has **no iterations**; it pulls work continuously.
- Unclear requirements, new technology, frequent change → **agile**. Stable, well-understood, repeatable work → **predictive**.
- "Doing agile" (following the practices) is not the same as "being agile" (living the values).
