## Key points

- A **project life cycle** is the series of activities or phases a project passes through from start to closure. A **phase** is a group of logically related activities that ends with one or more deliverables or outcomes.
- A **phase gate** is a review point where a go/no-go decision is made.
- A **development approach** is how the product, service or result is created and evolved: **predictive**, **adaptive** or **hybrid**. They form a spectrum.
- **Predictive** fits stable, clear requirements. **Adaptive** fits uncertain, changing requirements and needs frequent feedback. **Hybrid** mixes both, and most projects today benefit from it.
- **Delivery cadence** can be single, multiple, periodic or continuous.
- Risk and uncertainty are highest at the start of a project. The cost of changes rises as the project progresses.

## Project life cycle and phases

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.1

The **project life cycle** is the foundational framework for managing a project, whatever work or approach is used.

A **project phase** is a collection of logically related project activities that culminates in the completion of one or more deliverables and/or outcomes. Phases can be **sequential**, **transitional** or **overlapping**.

Phases have attributes such as name, number, duration, resource requirements, **entrance criteria** (what must be true to start the phase) and **exit criteria** (what must be true to finish it).

The project life cycle is **independent of the product life cycle**. A product lives on long after the project that created it.

## Phase gates

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.1

A **phase gate** usually sits at the end of a phase, before the next begins. It includes a review, also called a **stage gate, gate review, iteration review or decision point review**, to check that the exit criteria were met. Performance is compared with project and business documents, and a decision follows:

- continue to the next phase
- continue with modifications
- end the project or phase
- remain in the phase
- repeat the phase or parts of it
- park the project temporarily for a more urgent initiative

Gates can also be placed at the **start** of a phase (pre-phase gates) to confirm alignment, resources and planning before work begins.

## What changes over the life of a project

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.1, Figure 4-1

| Characteristic | At the start | Toward the end |
|---|---|---|
| Risk and uncertainty | Highest | Decrease as decisions are made and deliverables accepted |
| Cost of changes | Low | High |
| Stakeholders' ability to influence scope cheaply | Highest | Decreases |
| Cost and staffing (predictive projects) | Low | Peak during the work, then drop rapidly at the end |

This is why involving stakeholders and resolving uncertainty **early** is cheaper than doing so late.

## Development approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2

A **development approach** is the means used to create and evolve the product, service or result during the project life cycle. It defines how the project will be managed and executed.

Do not confuse it with the **development phase**, which is the stage of a life cycle where deliverables are actually built and tested.

The approach is usually chosen by the **project manager in consultation with the project management team**.

## Predictive approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.1

Also called **waterfall, plan-driven or traditional**.

- Best when **requirements are clear and stable early** and the scope can be fixed.
- Scope, schedule, cost, resources, quality and risks are defined in the early phases and expected to stay relatively stable.
- Most planning is done **up front**.
- Uses robust **change control**.
- The scope, schedule and cost baselines together are the **integrated baseline**.

Good fit: large investments, heavy regulation, work where iterating costs far more than it is worth (the build phase of construction), or regulated sectors such as healthcare where regulators review at phase gates. A typical predictive life cycle: feasibility, design, build, check, transfer, close.

**Predictive with incremental delivery.** The whole scope is still understood and planned up front, but the product is delivered in parts. Each increment adds to the previous one. Stakeholders get value earlier, while overall scope and timeline stay largely fixed.

## Adaptive approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.2

Also called **change-driven or agile**.

- Best when requirements and the technical solution are **highly uncertain and likely to change**.
- A clear **vision** is set at the start. Requirements are then progressively elaborated, changed or replaced based on feedback.
- Work is reviewed in a regular, **timeboxed** cadence so feedback can be absorbed.
- The whole team takes part in planning. In each iteration the team picks work from the prioritized **product backlog**, estimates it and builds it.
- Some agile methods use iterations of **1 to 4 weeks**, with a demonstration at the end of each.

Adaptive approaches can be both:

| | Iterative | Incremental |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | **Refine and improve** through repeated cycles | **Deliver in small usable pieces** that build on each other |
| Each cycle produces | A better version | An additional part that provides value |

**Flow-based scheduling** optimizes the flow of work instead of using fixed iterations. Two sources:

- **Kanban:** a visual workflow method that limits **work in process (WIP)**, based on lean principles, aiming at continuous delivery without overburdening the team.
- **Theory of constraints:** find and fix the single most limiting factor (the constraint) standing in the way of the goal.

**Method vs tool kit.** A method is a systematic series of steps. A tool kit is a collection of tools and techniques to choose from. PMI's **Disciplined Agile (DA)** is a tool kit. Team-level methods include Scrum, XP and Kanban; scaled approaches include SAFe, LeSS and Scrum of Scrums.

Good fit: design projects, new product development, and digital or software projects with evolving scope.

## The inverted triangle

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2, Figure 4-3

In a predictive project, **scope is fixed** and budget and schedule may have to vary to deliver it. In an adaptive project the triangle is inverted: **budget and schedule are fixed** (the team and the timeboxes) and **scope varies**.

| | Fixed | Variable |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive | Scope | Budget, schedule |
| Adaptive | Budget, schedule | Scope |

## Hybrid approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.3

A **combination of predictive and adaptive** elements. The 8th edition is clear that **most projects today benefit from a hybrid approach**: it gives flexibility for what is uncertain while keeping control over what is predictable.

Four common patterns:

1. **Adaptive first, then a predictive rollout**
2. **Adaptive and predictive used together** throughout the life cycle
3. **Largely predictive with an adaptive component**
4. **Largely adaptive with a predictive component**

Examples: a custom home (iterate on the design, then build once); software built adaptively and installed in a data center built predictively; a smart building; a hospital records system with predictive infrastructure and an adaptive user interface.

Disciplined Agile describes three levels: **Level 1** predictive dominates with some adaptive elements; **Level 2** both contribute significantly; **Level 3** adaptive dominates with some predictive elements.

## Choosing an approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.3

Factors fall into three groups. Different deliverables within one project may need different approaches.

| Factor | Points toward predictive | Points toward adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **Deliverables** | | |
| Degree of innovation | Familiar work | New or complex innovation |
| Requirements certainty | Well known | Unclear |
| Scope stability | Stable | Unstable |
| Ease of change | Changes are hard or costly | Changes are easy |
| Safety requirements | Rigorous | |
| Regulations | Significant oversight | |
| Feedback | | Frequent feedback is valuable |
| **Project** | | |
| Stakeholders | | Heavy involvement throughout |
| Schedule | Fixed end date | Value in delivering something early |
| Financing | | Uncertain funding |
| **Organization** | | |
| Structure | Functional, hierarchical | Network-oriented |
| Culture | Managing and directing, measuring against baselines | Embraces uncertainty, self-managed teams |
| Team size | Depends on scope | Smaller teams, often three to nine people |

## Delivery cadence

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.4

| Cadence | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Single delivery** | Everything is delivered at the end | A re-engineered process rolled out once |
| **Multiple deliveries** | Components delivered at different times, in sequence or in parallel | Drug development: trial phases, approval, launch |
| **Periodic deliveries** | Like multiple, but on a fixed regular schedule | Software delivered every two weeks |
| **Continuous delivery** | Tested increments flow to production on an ongoing basis, supported by automation | A web service updated many times a week |

Periodic deliveries are common in adaptive approaches.

> **Edition note.** The 7th edition treated this material as the "Development Approach and Life Cycle" performance domain. The 8th edition moves it into the Standard as "Project Life Cycles". The ideas are the same.

## Exam tips

- Stable, well-understood requirements, heavy regulation or safety: **predictive**. Unclear or changing requirements, need for feedback, innovation: **adaptive**. A mix of both kinds of work: **hybrid**.
- **Iterative** improves the same thing through repetition. **Incremental** adds new usable pieces. Agile does both.
- In adaptive projects, when something unexpected happens, **scope** flexes, not the timebox.
- A phase gate is a **decision point**. "Kill the project" is a valid outcome.
- Risk is highest at the **start**. The cost of change is highest at the **end**.
