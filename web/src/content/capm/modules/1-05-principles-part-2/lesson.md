## Key points

- **Be an Accountable Leader:** take ownership of the project's objectives, your decisions and your actions. Leadership is behavior, not position, and anyone on the project can show it.
- **Leadership is not authority.** Authority is a position of control granted by the organization. Leadership is inspiring and motivating others by example.
- **Integrate Sustainability Within All Project Areas:** meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- The **sustainability pyramid** ranks responses to negative impacts: avoid (best), minimize, restore, compensate (least desirable).
- **Build an Empowered Culture:** mutual trust, clear roles and team agreements let a diverse team and its stakeholders collaborate and contribute freely.

## Principle 4: Be an Accountable Leader

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 3.6

Projects need leadership more than routine operations do. They bring together organizations, departments and vendors that do not normally work together, the stakes are higher, and many people try to influence the outcome. That creates confusion and conflict. High-performing projects show leadership behaviors **more often and from more people** than low-performing ones.

**Accountable leadership** means being responsible for, and taking ownership of, the project's target objectives, the actions taken and the decisions made. When you accept or pull a piece of work, you are accountable for carrying it out.

Characteristics of an accountable leader:

| Characteristic | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Integrity, honesty and fairness** | Decisions follow moral principles, especially in hard situations, and aim at the common good. This builds trust |
| **Self-awareness** | Understanding your own motives, values, feelings and strengths, and how they drive your actions |
| **Respectfulness, humility and availability** | Being open to feedback, working *for* the team, supporting their needs and removing barriers. This is the basis of **servant leadership** |
| **Flexibility and adaptability** | Adapting leadership style to the situation and the audience without losing core values |
| **Shared leadership** | Leadership is not tied to one role. At different moments a team member or stakeholder may lead |

**Leadership vs authority**

| | Authority | Leadership |
|---|---|---|
| Source | A position granted by the organization | Behavior and example |
| Works through | Control | Inspiring and motivating |
| Who has it | The person in the role | Anyone on the project |

Accountable leaders also foster **psychological safety**: an environment where people can speak up, ask questions and admit mistakes without fear. They develop other leaders around them.

Results of this principle: better team performance, more trust and morale, better decisions, greater stakeholder confidence, and resilience under pressure.

> Example from the book: on a government megaproject, vendor teams clash over shift rotations. The conventional response is to enforce the contract and tell each vendor to sort out its own staff. An accountable leader instead brings the vendors together to find the root cause and agree on adjusted rotations, which resolves the conflict and protects quality and productivity.

## Principle 5: Integrate Sustainability Within All Project Areas

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 3.7

**Sustainability** means meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. In projects it means considering **people, the planet, society and value** in every area and every phase. Project managers, teams and sponsors are **jointly accountable** for it.

It aligns with the **triple bottom line**: social equity, environmental stewardship and economic prosperity (people, planet, profit).

**The sustainability pyramid.** For any negative side effect (an *externality*) the project creates, the options from most to least desirable are:

1. **Avoid** the negative outcome altogether
2. **Minimize** the negative outcome
3. **Restore** the impact
4. **Compensate / offset** for the negative outcome

**Where it shows up in a project**

- Organizational sustainability strategies may already apply as **EEFs** that impose requirements on the project.
- Sustainability **KPIs** can be written into the charter, business case, scope statement or contracts.
- Goals should be in the formal documents and confirmed by the **sponsor**. If they are not, the project manager should still raise the topic with stakeholders.

**Benefits**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Organizational | Employee satisfaction and retention, reputation and brand value, better governance, fewer legal costs |
| Operational | Process innovation, less operational waste |
| Financial | Lower material and energy costs, less waste, interest from sustainable-finance investors |
| Customer and stakeholder | Higher satisfaction, market share from demand for sustainable products, new markets |

**Challenges**

- It **broadens project management**: longer time horizons and more stakeholders, which can raise cost and complexity.
- **Sustainability risks** appear when societal, economic and environmental considerations are not balanced, for example choosing lowest cost over environmental care, or ignoring community impact.
- There are **no universal criteria** for measuring sustainability success, so each project needs a tailored approach.

Sustainability widens the orientation of project management in two directions: from the *process* life cycle to the *deliverable* and then the *outcomes* life cycle, and from *sponsor and team* to *all influential stakeholders* and then *community, society and posterity*.

> Example: a construction team could simply buy the cheapest materials, at a cost to the environment and health. Applying the principle, it still watches value and return, but plans materials carefully, picks sources with the least environmental impact (recycled concrete, wood), and involves the local community.

## Principle 6: Build an Empowered Culture

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 3.8

An empowered culture needs **mutual trust** among stakeholders and team members, and **full clarity** on roles, responsibilities, team agreements and guiding processes. With these in place, people contribute ideas freely and proactively, and collaboration produces more than the sum of individual efforts.

The book's headline for this principle: **stakeholders determine the success of projects.**

Areas where the principle works:

| Area | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **Diversity** | Different perspectives enrich the team. Internal staff, contractors, volunteers and third parties work together. Honoring diversity helps conflicts be managed efficiently |
| **Process definition** | The team defines the processes that let work get done, and engages stakeholders to understand and respond to their interests |
| **Interpersonal skills** | Initiative, integrity, honesty, collaboration, respect, empathy and confidence |
| **Knowledge of organizational structures** | Understanding how tasks and procedures fit together so individual contributions synchronize |
| **Team agreements** | Behavioral parameters and working norms set **by the team**, created **at the beginning** of the project, upheld by individual and team commitment |

Project teams often build their **own culture** within the cultures of the organizations involved. Remote and virtual teams face extra difficulty (work styles, time zones, no in-person contact), which makes an empowered culture even more important.

Empowered teams affect every domain. They can propose speeding up, slowing down or stopping activities; they help cut unplanned spending; they help define risk thresholds; and they shape how stakeholders are engaged.

## The six principles at a glance

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 3.1

| Principle | One-line essence | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| Adopt a Holistic View | See the project as a connected system | Proactive |
| Embed Quality Into Processes and Deliverables | Build quality in; prevent, improve, cut waste | Proactive |
| Be an Accountable Leader | Own decisions and outcomes; lead by example | Ownership |
| Build an Empowered Culture | Trust, clarity and team agreements | Ownership |
| Focus on Value | Outcomes and benefits over outputs | Value-driven |
| Integrate Sustainability Within All Project Areas | People, planet, society and value, for the long term | Value-driven |

## Exam tips

- A question where a team member (not the manager) steps up to guide the group is testing **shared leadership**. It is a good thing, not a challenge to authority.
- "Removes obstacles", "works for the team", "humility": **servant leadership**, part of Be an Accountable Leader.
- When conflict appears, PMI's preferred answer is to **collaborate and find the root cause**, not to enforce the contract or escalate first.
- Sustainability pyramid order: **avoid, minimize, restore, compensate**. Paying compensation is the weakest option.
- **Team agreements** (also called ground rules or a team charter) are written by the team itself, early in the project.
