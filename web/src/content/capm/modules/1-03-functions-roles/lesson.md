## Key points

- Projects are delivered by people fulfilling **functions**. One person can perform several functions, and one function can be shared by several people.
- Coordination can be **centralized** (a project manager leads, typical of predictive work), **decentralized** (the team self-organizes, typical of agile work) or a **hybrid**.
- The **project management team** may be one project manager or several people. What matters is the function, not the job title.
- A project management team needs five competencies: **ways of working, power skills, business acumen, results** and **social responsibilities**.
- The **sponsor, customer or product owner** provides decisions, resources and support that are beyond the project management team's authority.
- A **product life cycle** (introduction, growth, maturity, decline/retirement) usually contains many projects.

## Functions associated with projects

A function is a kind of contribution a project needs. PMI lists seven common ones. The list is not exhaustive, and the needs of the project decide which are used.

| Function | What it does | Who typically performs it |
|---|---|---|
| **Provide oversight and coordination** | Aligns efforts, removes obstacles, keeps the team focused. Can include all project management activities | Project manager, scrum master |
| **Solicit and manage feedback** | Brings in perspectives, direction and expectations from customers and end users | Customer, end user, product owner |
| **Facilitate and support** | Encourages participation and collaboration, helps the team reach consensus, resolve conflict and get through change | Project manager, scrum master, team lead, business analyst, change specialist |
| **Perform work** | Supplies the knowledge, skills and experience to produce the deliverables | Project team members |
| **Apply expertise** | Contributes specialized knowledge in a subject area, spots blind spots | Internal or external subject matter experts |
| **Provide organizational direction and insight** | Prioritizes requirements or scope by value, dependencies and risk; sets the course | Portfolio manager, sponsor, product owner |
| **Provide resources** | Secures funding, people, physical resources and authority; allocates them to the team | Sponsor, portfolio manager, functional and resource managers |

Two details worth knowing:

- **Feedback** is needed more in adaptive and hybrid approaches, because the team is discovering the product in increments.
- **Providing resources** has two sides. Sponsors and portfolio managers *secure* resources and champion the project with senior management. Functional and resource managers *allocate* the right people and equipment to the team.

## How the work is coordinated

- **Centralized coordination:** a designated project manager or similar role leads and guides. Common in predictive projects.
- **Decentralized coordination:** team members self-organize and self-manage. Common in agile projects.
- **Hybrid coordination:** centralized overall, with self-organizing teams for parts of the work.

Whichever is used, it is the collective effort of the project team that delivers the outcomes, benefits and value.

## The project management team

The **project management team** may be only a project manager, or several people who share project management activities. It guides the team to achieve the goals and deliver value, and performs functions such as facilitating, mentoring and managing the processes.

**The title is not what matters.** In some organizations a functional manager runs the project, or the role is called project lead. In agile environments a product owner, scrum master, agile coach or team lead may carry part of the project management responsibility. The essence of project management lies in the nature of the project, not in the title of the person leading it.

**Involvement over time.** The project management team is ideally involved from initiation to closing. In some organizations it also takes part in feasibility analysis and business case development before the project starts, and in benefit analysis after it ends.

**Sphere of influence.** The project management team works with many parties: the project team, the sponsor, the PMO, portfolio and program managers, functional and operational managers, customers, users, suppliers and regulators. It negotiates with other managers for scarce resources, funding priorities and deliverables.

**Leadership style.** Leaders should be versatile. **Situational leadership** means adjusting the style to the needs of the team, the urgency of the task and the complexity of the project, for example being more directive in a critical phase and more supportive when the team can work autonomously.

## Five competencies of a project management team

| Competency | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Ways of working** | Knowing and applying standards, methods and frameworks | Choosing predictive, adaptive or hybrid; brainstorming; change management techniques |
| **Power skills** | People skills | Emotional intelligence, critical thinking, motivation, negotiation, conflict resolution |
| **Business acumen** | Strategic thinking and understanding the organization's context | Project selection criteria, industry knowledge, business case, financial analysis |
| **Results** | Actually getting the work done and delivering value | Problem solving (root cause analysis), gaining authority, handling organizational politics |
| **Social responsibilities** | Making decisions aligned with the common good | Giving back to the profession, respecting ethical, cultural and social norms, self-development |

> **Edition note.** The first three are the **PMI Talent Triangle**: Ways of Working, Power Skills and Business Acumen. Older material calls them technical project management, leadership, and strategic and business management. The 8th edition adds Results and Social Responsibilities around them.

**Developing the team.** The project management team also builds a learning culture: mixing seniority levels, mentoring and coaching, giving people stretch tasks with constructive feedback, and capturing lessons learned. Functional managers, the PMO or individuals themselves may share this responsibility.

**Technology and AI.** Tools and AI can automate routine tasks, forecast risks and support decisions. The project management team must make sure the data is accurate and the output is not biased, keep monitoring that the tools serve the project, and work with the IT department to protect project information. In some organizations the PMO owns tool selection.

## Sponsor, customer or product owner

These roles provide **decision leadership outside the project management team's authority**. They:

- communicate the organization's vision, goals and expectations
- keep the project aligned with business objectives
- facilitate executive-level decisions
- help secure resources
- advocate for the team
- remove obstacles and resolve issues beyond the project management team's authority
- monitor progress so the intended benefits are realized

Their active involvement raises the chance of success. An absent sponsor is a real risk to the project.

## Project team, end users and other stakeholders

The **project team** is the set of individuals who perform the project's work and are directly responsible for achieving its objectives. Its size and composition depend on the project's type, scale and complexity.

**End users and other key stakeholders** (customers, regulators, influencers) should be engaged in continuous dialogue. Their feedback is gathered throughout the life cycle and checked through repeated verification and validation, so the product actually meets their needs. A deliverable that nobody uses delivers no value.

## Product management and the product life cycle

**Product management** integrates people, data, processes and business systems to create, develop and maintain a product or service throughout its life cycle. It is a separate discipline from project management.

The **product life cycle** is the series of phases a product goes through:

1. **Introduction**
2. **Growth**
3. **Maturity**
4. **Decline / retirement**

A product life cycle is usually much longer than any project. Projects and programs can be started at any point in it: one project creates the product, later ones add features or revise it, and a final one retires it.

| | Product | Project |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Long-lived, through its whole life cycle | Temporary |
| Managed by | Product manager / product owner | Project manager / project management team |
| Provides | Vision and strategy for the product | Execution: managing work, dependencies and resources |

The disciplines relate in several ways: programs or projects inside a product life cycle, product management inside a portfolio, product responsibilities inside a program or project, and product management across several programs and projects.

Two roles bridge product and project work:

- **Product owner:** keeps the team focused on valuable features by maintaining and prioritizing the **product backlog**.
- **Business analyst:** gathers and documents requirements so that project scope and product functionality match business needs.

## Exam tips

- "Who secures funding and removes obstacles above the project manager's authority?" The **sponsor**.
- "Who prioritizes the backlog?" The **product owner**.
- "Who assigns people from a department to the project?" The **functional (resource) manager**.
- If a question describes a self-organizing team, the coordination is decentralized and the approach is adaptive.
- Do not assume the project manager must do everything. PMI describes functions that can be shared, and titles vary.
- A product life cycle contains projects, not the other way round.
