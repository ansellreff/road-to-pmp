## Key points

- A **process** turns **inputs** into **outputs** using **tools and techniques** (ITTO).
- A predictive project flows: **business case → project charter → project management plan → deliverables → accepted deliverables → closure**.
- The **project charter** authorizes the project. The **project management plan** says how it will be executed, monitored and controlled, and closed.
- **Work performance data** (raw) becomes **work performance information** (analyzed against the plan), which becomes **work performance reports** (presented for decisions).
- A **change request** is a formal proposal to modify a document, deliverable or baseline. It may be a corrective action, preventive action, defect repair or update.
- A deliverable is first **verified** (checked for correctness by the team) and then **accepted** (approved by the customer or authorized stakeholder).

## What a process is

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 2

A **process** is a set of activities that takes **inputs**, applies **tools and techniques**, and produces **outputs**. PMI shortens this to **ITTO**.

| Part | Question it answers | Example for "Initiate Project or Phase" |
|---|---|---|
| **Inputs** | What do I need before I start? | Business case, agreements |
| **Tools and techniques** | How do I do it? | Expert judgment, interviews, meetings |
| **Outputs** | What do I have when I finish? | Project charter, assumption log |

The **output of one process is usually the input of another**. That chain is what makes a project "flow". The 8th edition lists sample ITTOs for each of its 40 processes and says plainly that they are illustrative, not mandatory.

Two inputs appear in almost every process: **enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)** and **organizational process assets (OPAs)**, from module 1.2. Two outputs appear in many: **project management plan updates** and **project document updates**.

## How a predictive project flows

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.6; PMBOK 8, Guide Table 2-1

1. **Before the project.** The sponsor's organization prepares the **business documents**: the business case and the benefits management plan.
2. **Initiating.** The **project charter** is issued and the project manager is authorized. Stakeholders are identified.
3. **Planning.** Each domain produces its plan and baselines. They are combined into one **project management plan**.
4. **Executing.** The team does the work in the plan, producing **deliverables** and **work performance data**.
5. **Monitoring and controlling.** Data is compared with the plan to give **work performance information** and **reports**. Variances lead to **change requests**. Approved changes go back to executing. Deliverables are **verified**, then **accepted**.
6. **Closing.** The final product is transitioned, lessons are recorded, and a **final report** is written.

Monitoring and controlling runs alongside the other steps, not after them.

## Business documents and agreements

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Business documents; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Business case; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Benefits management plan; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Agreements

**Business documents** are created **before** the project and looked at again **after** it, which is why they are neither project documents nor part of the project management plan.

| Document | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Business case** | A documented **economic feasibility study** that establishes whether the benefits are worth the investment. It lists the objectives and reasons for the project, may produce a go/no-go decision, and is used at the end to measure success |
| **Benefits management plan** | Explains **how and when the benefits will be delivered** and how they will be measured: target benefits, strategic alignment, timeframe for realizing them |

- The **sponsor** is generally accountable for developing and maintaining the business case.
- The **project manager** keeps the charter, the plan and the benefits management plan aligned with it.

**Agreements** define the initial intentions of a project: a contract, service-level agreement (SLA), memorandum of understanding (MOU), letter of agreement, purchase order, even an email or a verbal agreement.

## The project charter

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Project charter; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.6.1

The **project charter** is a document issued by the **project initiator or sponsor** that **formally authorizes the existence of the project** and gives the project manager the **authority to apply organizational resources** to project activities.

- It links the project to the business case and the organization's strategy.
- The project manager should be identified and assigned **as early as possible**, ideally while the charter is being developed and **always before planning begins**.
- The charter can be developed by the sponsor, or by the project manager working with the initiating entity.

## The project management plan and baselines

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Project management plan; PMBOK 8, Glossary (baseline)

The **project management plan** is the document that describes **how the project will be executed, monitored and controlled, and closed**. It is the basis for all project decisions and a **living document** that is expected to change.

It typically contains:

- **subsidiary management plans:** scope, requirements, schedule, financial, quality, resource, communications, risk, procurement or sourcing strategy, stakeholder engagement, change management, configuration management
- **baselines:** the scope baseline, schedule baseline and cost baseline

A **baseline** is the approved version of a work product. It is changed **only through formal change control** and is what actual results are compared against. The three baselines together are the **integrated baseline** (also called the performance measurement baseline).

## Project documents

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Project documents

**Project documents** are created throughout the project to help manage it, but they are **not part of the project management plan**. Examples: assumption log, change log, issue log, lessons learned register, project schedule, project scope statement, requirements documentation, risk register, stakeholder register.

| | Project management plan | Project documents |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | **How** the project will be managed | Day-to-day **records and working information** |
| Examples | Risk management plan, schedule baseline | Risk register, project schedule |
| Changed by | Formal change control for baselined parts | Updated by the team as work proceeds |

A common exam trap: the **risk management plan** is in the project management plan, but the **risk register** is a project document. The same goes for the *schedule management plan* (plan) and the *project schedule* (document).

## Work performance data, information and reports

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Work performance data; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Work performance information; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Work performance reports

| | What it is | Example | Where it arises |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Work performance data** | **Raw** observations and measurements taken while work is performed | "Activity A is 60% complete. $40,000 spent so far" | Executing |
| **Work performance information** | Data **analyzed and compared with the plan**, so it has context | "We are 10% behind schedule and $5,000 over budget" | Monitoring and controlling |
| **Work performance reports** | Information **compiled and presented** to generate decisions, actions or awareness | A status report, a dashboard, a burndown chart | Monitoring and controlling |

The order is always **data → information → reports**. Data alone says nothing about whether the project is on track; it needs the comparison with the plan.

## Change requests

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Change requests; PMBOK 8, Guide Figure 2-11

A **change request** is a **formal proposal to modify a document, deliverable or baseline**. Anyone can raise one, from inside or outside the project.

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Corrective action** | Bring current performance **back in line** with the plan | Adding staff to recover a schedule delay |
| **Preventive action** | Make sure **future** performance stays in line | Extra training before a risky task |
| **Defect repair** | Fix a deliverable that **does not meet requirements** | Reworking a faulty component |
| **Update** | Change a plan or document to reflect new ideas or content | Adding a newly agreed requirement |

Each request is analyzed for impact and then **approved, rejected or deferred**. The outcome is recorded in the **change log**. Approved change requests are implemented through executing. Module 2.2 covers the change control process.

## From deliverable to accepted deliverable

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Verified deliverables; PMBOK 8, Guide section 4, Accepted deliverables

1. **Deliverable:** produced by the team during executing.
2. **Verified deliverable:** checked and confirmed for **correctness** against requirements. This is an internal quality check.
3. **Accepted deliverable:** validated against the acceptance criteria and formally **approved by the customer** or another authorized person.

| | Verify | Validate / accept |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Did we build it **right**? | Did we build the **right thing**? |
| Who | The project team | The customer or authorized stakeholder |
| Result | Verified deliverable | Accepted deliverable |

The person who will accept the deliverables should be involved **early**, giving feedback while changes are still cheap.

## Tools and techniques you will see everywhere

@source PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Expert judgment; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Data gathering; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Data analysis; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Decision-making; PMBOK 8, Guide section 5, Meetings

| Tool | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Expert judgment** | Judgment from any person or group with specialized education, knowledge, skill, experience or training |
| **Data gathering** | Collecting data: brainstorming, focus groups, interviews, checklists |
| **Data analysis** | Turning data into insight: alternatives analysis, cost-benefit analysis, root cause analysis, trend analysis, variance analysis |
| **Decision-making** | Voting by **unanimity** (everyone agrees), **majority** (more than 50%) or **plurality** (the largest block, even without a majority); autocratic decision-making (one person decides) |
| **Interpersonal and team skills** | Facilitation, conflict management, meeting management, emotional intelligence |
| **Meetings** | Kickoffs, status updates, issue resolution, workshops, retrospectives |
| **Project management information system (PMIS)** | The software tools used to plan, track and share project information |

## Governance models

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.2

Too much governance wastes resources. Too little causes alignment and performance problems.

| | Structured governance | Self-governance |
|---|---|---|
| Typical of | More **predictive** projects | More **adaptive** projects |
| Who governs | Executive sponsor, PMO leader, a governance board and the project manager | The team itself; project management responsibilities are shared |
| Main risk | Bureaucracy | Fragmented decisions that conflict with each other |

## Governing by the right measures

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.3; PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.5

Effective governance needs three things: **target metrics** linked to strategic goals, **signaling mechanisms** that warn early, and **feedback mechanisms** so decision-makers can learn.

| | Leading indicator | Lagging indicator |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | **Predicts** changes or trends before they happen | **Measures** what has already happened |
| Ease of measuring | Harder | Easier |
| Examples | Size of the backlog in progress, stakeholders not engaged, unclear success criteria | Deliverables completed, schedule or cost variance, resources consumed |
| Use | Prevent problems | Confirm results, find correlations |

Leading indicators are preferred whenever possible, because they let you act before rework is needed.

**SMART criteria** for goals and metrics: **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic, **T**ime-bound.

Good governance watches **outcome** measures (return on investment, due-date performance, value) and not just **input** measures such as resource utilization or training completed.

## Two extras for predictive environments

@source PMBOK 8, Guide 2.1.4

- **Escalation:** taking a decision or obstacle to people with more authority. Useful in hierarchical organizations where authority sits outside the team.
- **Investment control:** formal stewardship of the project's funding, with decision points to confirm that the next round of investment still makes business sense. Common in public companies, government and lender-financed construction.

## Exam tips

- "Which document authorizes the project?" The **project charter**. "Who issues it?" The **sponsor or initiator**, not the project manager.
- "Which document describes how the project will be managed?" The **project management plan**.
- Data is **raw**, information is **analyzed**, reports are **presented**. If an answer mentions a comparison with the plan, it is information.
- A **baseline** can change only through **formal change control**.
- The business case is written **before** the project and is not a project document.
- **Plurality** is not majority: the largest group wins even with less than half the votes.
- Verified comes **before** accepted. The team verifies; the customer accepts.
