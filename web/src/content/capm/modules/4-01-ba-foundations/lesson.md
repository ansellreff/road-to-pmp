## Key points

- **Business analysis** finds the **business need**, recommends a **solution**, and defines and manages the **requirements** for it.
- The business analyst is responsible for the **product**. The project manager is responsible for the **project**.
- Anyone can do business analysis. "Business analyst" names the **work**, not a job title.
- **Product requirements** come in four types: **business, stakeholder, solution** (functional and nonfunctional) and **transition**.
- **Project** and **quality** requirements belong to **project management**, not business analysis.
- Business analysis covers the **whole product life cycle**: it starts **before** a project exists and continues **after** it ends.
- PMI's guide has **35 processes** in **6 knowledge areas** and **6 process groups**.
- A business analyst **leads and influences without authority**, so relationships and trust matter most.

## What business analysis is

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.1.7.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.1.7.2

**Business analysis** is the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to:

1. **Determine** problems and opportunities
2. **Identify business needs** and **recommend viable solutions**, supporting strategic decisions
3. **Elicit, analyze, specify, communicate and manage requirements** and other product information
4. **Define benefits** and how value will be measured, and analyze the results

In short: the activities that support delivering **solutions that match business objectives** and keep providing value.

A **business analyst** is **anyone who performs this work**, whatever the job title and however much of the job it is. Titles vary: systems analyst, process analyst, requirements analyst, product owner. A project manager, an architect or a whole agile team can do business analysis too.

## Why business analysis matters

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.1.3

Organizations are often tempted to provide a solution **before they understand the situation**. Business analysis finds and fixes the **root cause**, not the symptoms.

| Benefit | How |
|---|---|
| **Addresses business needs** | A needs assessment comes first; the solution is based on the real problem |
| **Manages risk and reduces rework** | Well-understood requirements mean fewer change requests later |
| **Reduces product defects** | Missing or misunderstood requirements cause defects. Defects found after release are far more costly: recalls, lawsuits, lost confidence. |
| **Increases stakeholder satisfaction** | The product is accepted and actually used |

Taking time for business analysis **saves time, reduces cost and lowers risk** in the long run, even though it looks like extra work at the start.

Three areas an organization can strengthen: **people** (skilled analysts), **processes** (standard practice across initiatives) and **culture** (executives who value the practice).

## Business analyst and project manager

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 3.3.2; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.2.3; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 3.3.1

The two roles are often confused. The difference is the **objective**.

| | Business analyst | Project manager |
|---|---|---|
| **Responsible for** | Successful delivery of the **product** | Successful delivery of the **project** |
| **Scope concerned** | **Product scope:** the features and functions of the solution | **Project scope:** the work needed to deliver it |
| **Requirements owned** | Product requirements | Project and quality requirements |
| **Stakeholders engaged in order to** | Get the information needed to build the right solution | Manage the project |
| **Success measured by** | The solution delivering its **intended benefits**, customer satisfaction, business objectives met | Product and project quality, **timeline, budget**, customer satisfaction |
| **Time horizon** | The whole **product life cycle** | The **project life cycle** |

- A business analyst **may lead, but does not manage project resources**. Allocation, scheduling and work progress, including the analysts' own, belong to the project manager.
- Both roles engage stakeholders, for different purposes. They should **work together** when a stakeholder problem affects the solution.
- Research cited by PMI found that organizations with **strong collaboration** between the two roles deliver projects more successfully.
- One person may hold both roles (a **hybrid** role). That person must keep the two sets of priorities apart.

## Products, projects and their life cycles

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.2.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.2.2

- A **product** is something produced that is quantifiable. It can be **tangible or intangible**: a device, a process, a service, an organizational structure.
- A **solution** is what is produced to deliver measurable business value. It may be a new product, part of a product, or an enhancement.
- Products **evolve through projects**: project 1 creates version 1, project 2 creates version 2, and so on.

| | Product life cycle | Project life cycle |
|---|---|---|
| **Covers** | Concept, delivery, growth, maturity, maintenance, **retirement** | Initiation to closure of one project |
| **Length** | Long | Temporary |
| **Relationship** | Contains **one or more** project life cycles | Sits inside a product life cycle |

Where business analysis fits:

- **Before a project:** a **needs assessment** gives the strategic reason for investing. This is **pre-project** work.
- **During a project:** requirements are elicited, analyzed and managed.
- **After a project:** the product is **evaluated** to see whether it delivers value and whether another project is needed.

## Types of requirements

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.1.7.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Figure 1-1

A **requirement** is a condition or capability that must be present in a product, service or result to satisfy a business need.

**Product requirements** (business analysis):

| Type | Describes | Example for an online store |
|---|---|---|
| **Business requirement** | The **higher-level need** of the organization: **why** the work is being done. Usually defined **before** the project starts. | Increase online sales by 15% next year |
| **Stakeholder requirement** | What a **stakeholder or group** needs | Customers want to pay without creating an account |
| **Solution requirement** | The **features, functions and characteristics** of the product | See the next two rows |
| ↳ **Functional** | What the product **does**: behaviors, actions, processes | The system accepts card payments |
| ↳ **Nonfunctional** | **How well** it works: reliability, security, performance, safety | Payment is confirmed within 3 seconds |
| **Transition requirement** | **Temporary** capabilities needed to move from the current state to the future state | Convert existing customer data; train support staff |

**Not product requirements** (project management):

| Type | Describes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Project requirement** | Actions, processes or conditions the **project** must meet | Milestone dates, contractual obligations |
| **Quality requirement** | Criteria to **validate** that a deliverable is complete | Tests, certifications, validations |

How they connect:

- One business requirement is supported by **many** stakeholder and solution requirements.
- One stakeholder requirement is supported by **many** solution requirements.
- Transition requirements are **no longer needed** once the transition is complete.
- A product requirement should be **independent of the design** of the solution.
- Requirements can be **unstated**: stakeholders may not know what they need until they see a prototype. This is why several elicitation techniques are used.

**Nonfunctional requirement versus quality requirement.** A nonfunctional requirement is about the quality of the **product**. A quality requirement is about validating the **project's deliverables and processes**.

## Product information and stakeholders

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.1.7.5; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.1.7.7

**Product information** is everything business analysis creates and uses about the product: business goals and objectives, requirements, analysis models, backlogs, user stories, acceptance criteria, product scope, product risks, assumptions, constraints, dependencies and issues.

A **stakeholder** in business analysis includes anyone affected, or who thinks they are affected, by activities and decisions about the **solution**.

- The stakeholder register must be **kept up to date**. A missed stakeholder usually means **missed requirements**.
- Stakeholders may be occasional contributors, subject matter experts, decision makers or the sponsor.
- Different stakeholders can have **competing requirements**. Sorting these out is part of the analyst's work.

## Business value

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.2.4

**Business value** is the **net quantifiable benefit** from a business endeavor.

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Tangible** | Money, facilities, equity, equipment |
| **Intangible** | Goodwill, brand recognition, public benefit, compliance, capabilities |

- Benefits are stated as **measurable business objectives**, for example "increase revenue by US$1 million next year". These objectives are the base for business requirements and everything below them.
- Decide **before the project starts** what will be measured and how, and take a **baseline**.
- Benefits often appear **well after** the project ends. A **leading indicator** such as customer satisfaction can be measured sooner.
- **Customer value** and business value usually match, but not always. Focusing only on customer value is risky if the business gets nothing in return.
- A project's success should be tied to whether it **delivered its intended business value**.

## The six process groups

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.3.2; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Figure 1-4

A **process group** is a logical grouping of processes. Process groups are **not phases**: they repeat, and no order is fixed.

| Process group | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Defining and Aligning** | Investigate whether a new or changed product is viable; align products and projects with strategy. Happens **before** a project and again **after** it. |
| **Initiating** | Define the objectives and apply resources to a project or phase |
| **Planning** | Decide the best approach for the business analysis work; analyze stakeholders |
| **Executing** | Elicit, analyze, model, define, verify, validate, prioritize and approve product information |
| **Monitoring and Controlling** | Assess the impact of proposed changes; assess business analysis performance; keep stakeholders engaged |
| **Releasing** | Decide whether all or part of a solution should be released, and obtain acceptance |

Two differ from project management's five: **Defining and Aligning** is added at the front, and **Releasing** replaces Closing.

## The six knowledge areas

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.3.3; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Table 1-2; Business Analysis for Practitioners, How This Practice Guide Is Organized

A **knowledge area** is a set of processes for a particular function. The 35 processes are spread over six.

| Knowledge area | Purpose | Processes |
|---|---|---|
| **Needs Assessment** | Analyze the problem or opportunity to understand what is needed to reach the desired future state | 7 |
| **Stakeholder Engagement** | Identify and analyze those with an interest in the solution; decide how to collaborate and communicate | 7 |
| **Elicitation** | Plan, prepare, conduct and confirm elicitation to obtain information | 4 |
| **Analysis** | Examine, break down and clarify information to understand, complete and improve it | 9 |
| **Traceability and Monitoring** | Trace, approve and assess changes to product information | 4 |
| **Solution Evaluation** | Validate a solution to see how well it meets the need and delivers value | 4 |

How they relate:

- **Stakeholder Engagement** runs through everything.
- **Needs Assessment** is the basis for Elicitation, Analysis, and Traceability and Monitoring.
- **Elicitation, Analysis, and Traceability and Monitoring** tend to happen **at the same time**.
- **Solution Evaluation** examines their results and may trigger a **new needs assessment**.

**Tailoring** means choosing which processes, techniques and deliverables fit this particular effort, and how detailed and formal they should be. Not every process is needed every time.

> **On the exam.** The outline also names *Business Analysis for Practitioners: A Practice Guide*, Second Edition. It arranges the same work in **five domains**, each with three practices: **Business Value Assessment** (needs assessment), **Business Analysis Planning** (stakeholder engagement and planning), **Solution Refinement** (elicitation, analysis, and traceability), **Organizational Transition and Solution Evaluation**, and **Business Analysis Stewardship** (effectiveness, capability and integrity of the analyst's own work). Its people are called **business analysis practitioners**.

## The people a business analyst works with

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 3.3.3.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Glossary (Product Owner); PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Glossary (Product Manager); Effective Project Management, Chapter 7, Attendees

| Role | What they are | Internal or external |
|---|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Provides resources and support; **champions** the product and project. The analyst works with the sponsor to define the problem and acts as the sponsor's advocate. | Internal |
| **Product owner** | Has **decision-making authority to prioritize** what goes into a product | Internal |
| **Product manager** | Responsible for the product's **customer and market success** | Internal |
| **Process owner / process manager** | Responsible for a **business process** that gives input to, or receives output from, the solution. The solution must fit the process, or the process must change. | Internal |
| **Functional manager** | Manages a business area; can remove roadblocks and provide subject matter experts | Internal |
| **Subject matter expert (SME)** | An expert in a particular subject; the main source during elicitation and analysis | Either |
| **Project manager** | Leads the team that delivers the project; the analyst's closest partner | Internal |
| **Project team / product team** | The people who do the work. Everyone on the project team is on the product team, but **not the reverse**. | Internal |
| **Quality control (testers)** | Know existing products in detail; good reviewers of requirements for testability | Internal |
| **Governance** | Legal, risk, compliance, change control boards, the PMO | Internal |
| **Customers and users** | Those the solution is built for | Either |
| **Government and regulators** | Set rules the product must follow | External |
| **Vendors and suppliers** | Provide parts of the solution | External |

Why identify these roles at all? **A stakeholder who is overlooked is a set of requirements that will be missed.**

The business analyst leads and influences these people **without supervisory authority**. This is one of the hardest parts of the role. Authority is replaced by **trust, honesty, integrity and transparency**, which is why the analyst aims to be a **trusted advisor** to the business and a **liaison** to technical teams.

## Business analysis in predictive and adaptive projects

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 3.3.3.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 3.2.1.1

| | Predictive | Adaptive (agile) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who does it** | Usually a named business analyst | Often **spread across the team**; there may be no "business analyst" title |
| **When requirements are defined** | Mostly up front | Progressively, a little before each iteration |
| **Typical form** | Requirements documents, models | Backlog, user stories, acceptance criteria |
| **Working with the product owner** | The analyst supports the sponsor and business | The analyst often helps the **product owner refine the backlog** |

A Scrum team has a scrum master, a product owner and a development team, and no business analyst role. **The work of business analysis is still done.** It is simply shared, and the analyst's responsibilities shift to what the team needs.

## Skills of a business analyst

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 3.4

| Category | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Analytical skills** | Creative and conceptual thinking, problem solving, decision making, systems thinking, research |
| **Expert judgment** | Organizational, industry and product knowledge; business acumen; political and cultural awareness |
| **Communication skills** | Active listening, facilitation, verbal and nonverbal communication, professional writing, relationship building |
| **Personal skills** | Adaptability, ethics, objectivity, self-awareness, time management |
| **Leadership skills** | Change agent, negotiation, trusted advisor |
| **Tool knowledge** | Collaboration, reporting, requirements management and modeling tools |

Communication skills are among the **most critical**, because of the number of relationships and the amount of information involved.

## Exam tips

- **Product** points to the business analyst. **Project** points to the project manager.
- "**Why** is the organization doing this?" is a **business requirement**.
- "What the system **does**" is **functional**. "**How well**" (speed, security, reliability) is **nonfunctional**.
- Data conversion and training are **transition requirements**, and they are temporary.
- Milestone dates and contractual obligations are **project requirements**, not product requirements.
- Needs assessment is **pre-project** work, in the **Defining and Aligning** process group.
- The business analysis process groups have **Releasing**, not Closing.
- A stakeholder was missed: expect **missing requirements**. Update the register.
- No business analyst on an agile team does **not** mean no business analysis.
- The business analyst **does not** assign or schedule project resources.
- Who prioritizes what goes into the product? The **product owner**.
