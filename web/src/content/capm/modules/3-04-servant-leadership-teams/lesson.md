## Key points

- Agile leaders are **servant leaders**: they lead by **serving the team**, coaching instead of controlling. PMI's current agile guide calls this **supportive leadership**.
- A servant leader works in this order: **purpose, people, process**.
- The leader's main jobs: **facilitate**, **remove impediments**, and **pave the way** for the team.
- Agile teams are **small (three to nine people)**, **cross-functional**, **self-organizing** and ideally **100% dedicated**.
- **T-shaped** people (generalizing specialists) have one deep skill plus the ability to help in others.
- **Multitasking** costs **20% to 40%** of productivity.
- **Psychological safety** means people can speak up, ask questions and admit mistakes without fear. It is the most important factor in team effectiveness.

## Servant leadership

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.3

**Servant leadership** (called **supportive leadership** in PMI's current agile guide) is leading **through service to the team**: understanding and meeting the needs and development of team members so the team can perform at its best.

The leader approaches the work in this order:

| Order | Focus | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Purpose** | Work with the team to define the **why**, so everyone can rally around the goal |
| 2 | **People** | Help create an environment where **everyone can succeed** and contribute |
| 3 | **Process** | Do not chase a "perfect" agile process. **Look for results**: finished value delivered often, and regular reflection. |

Characteristics of a servant leader:

- promoting **self-awareness**,
- **listening**,
- **serving** those on the team,
- **helping people grow**,
- **coaching, not controlling**,
- promoting **safety, respect and trust**,
- promoting the **energy and intelligence of others**.

Servant leadership is not unique to agile, but it fits the agile mindset closely. It rests on a **growth mindset**: the belief that people can learn new skills.

| | Traditional manager | Servant leader |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions | Makes them for the team | Helps the team make them |
| Work assignment | Assigns tasks | The team chooses its own tasks |
| Problems | Solves them personally | Removes obstacles so the team can solve them |
| Stance | "The team works for me" | "I work for the team" |
| Tracks | Individual status | Team outcomes and flow |

## What a servant leader does

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.1

| Responsibility | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Facilitate** | Shift from **managing coordination** to **facilitating collaboration**. Help everyone think and work at their best; be an impartial bridge builder, not the decision-maker. | Running a workshop so the team finds its own solution |
| **Remove organizational impediments** | Look hard at processes that slow the team and work to **streamline** them | Working with another department to reduce required documentation, or shortening a six-week release process |
| **Pave the way for others' contributions** | Get the team what it needs to do its best work | Arranging a team space, or agreeing with management that the team works on one project at a time |

Other things a servant leader does:

- **Educates stakeholders** about why and how to be agile.
- **Builds the team's capability** through mentoring, encouragement and training, even if that means a member later moves on to a bigger role.
- Helps find the **root causes** of recurring problems.
- **Celebrates** team successes.
- Practices **emotional intelligence**: self-awareness, active listening, nondefensive responses.
- **Shields the team** from interruptions and outside pressure, so it can focus.

"We lead teams by standing behind them."

## The project manager in an agile project

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.2; Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.3

Many agile frameworks **do not describe a project manager role**. Where one exists, the role changes:

| Predictive | Agile |
|---|---|
| At the **center** of coordination | **Supporting** the team from the side |
| Plans and assigns the work | The team plans and organizes its own work |
| Tracks and reports status for the team | Makes progress visible; the team owns it |
| Directs | **Coaches**, facilitates collaboration, aligns stakeholder needs |

- In high-change projects there is **more complexity than one person can manage**, so responsibility is **distributed to the team**, the people who have the knowledge to do the work.
- A project manager still adds value on things **outside the team's reach**: cross-team dependencies, external stakeholders, vendors, risk, regulatory or contractual compliance, and communication with sponsors.
- In larger settings, project managers often take outward-facing, coordinating roles and remove **organization-level** impediments.

## What an agile team looks like

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.4.1; Agile Practice Guide, 4.4.2

| Attribute | Why |
|---|---|
| **Small: three to nine members** | Keeps communication manageable without formal structures |
| **Cross-functional** | The team has **all the skills** needed to deliver finished work, with no outside dependencies |
| **Self-organizing (self-managing)** | Team members decide **who does what** and how |
| **100% dedicated** | No multitasking, so the fastest possible throughput |
| **Collaborating face to face** | Better communication and knowledge sharing; in person or by video |
| **Stable** | People learn to depend on one another and keep their knowledge |
| **Mix of generalists and specialists** | Specialists bring depth; generalists bring flexibility |

Three common roles:

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Cross-functional team members** | Produce the working product; decide how to do the work; share ownership of the outcome |
| **Product owner** | Guides the direction of the product; **ranks the work by business value**; works with the team daily |
| **Team facilitator** | The servant leader: facilitates, coaches, removes impediments. May be called scrum master, team lead, coach or project manager. |

Agile optimizes the **flow of value**, not keeping everyone busy. Ways the team works together:

- **Pairing:** two people work on one item.
- **Swarming:** several people focus on one item until it is finished.
- **Mobbing:** the whole team works on one item together.

These prevent **mini-waterfalls**, where the team does all the analysis, then all the design, then all the building inside an iteration, and discovers its wrong assumptions too late.

## Generalizing specialists: T-shaped skills

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.6

| | Skills | Effect on the team |
|---|---|---|
| **I-shaped** person | Deep in **one** area; rarely contributes outside it | Becomes a **bottleneck** when that skill is needed, and idle when it is not |
| **T-shaped** person (generalizing specialist) | Deep in one area **plus** supporting skills in others, and good at collaborating | Can help wherever the work is stuck; fewer handoffs |

- One person's output is **not** what matters. What matters is the **throughput of the whole team**: **flow efficiency**, not resource efficiency.
- People become T-shaped through close collaboration, by routinely helping each other finish work.

## Dedicated teams and the cost of multitasking

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.1

When people are split across projects they must **task-switch**, and each switch costs time and attention.

- Teams lose **20% to 40%** of their productivity to task-switching.
- A person split between two projects is **not** 50% on each. They are effectively only **20% to 40%** on each.
- People make **more mistakes** when they multitask.
- When everyone is 100% on one team, the team has the **fastest possible throughput** and can predict its delivery.

If a specialist can only join part-time, set clear expectations with everyone about how much time and for how long. Part-time assignments are a **risk**.

## Team spaces and remote teams

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.7; Agile Practice Guide, 4.7.2

| Arrangement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Colocated** | The whole team works in the same place. Best for communication. |
| **Distributed** | Several cross-functional teams, each in a different location |
| **Dispersed** | Each team member works from a different location |
| **Remote** | Individuals working from separate locations, not a shared office |
| **Hybrid-location** | A mix of on-site and remote members |

Ideas about team space that are often tested:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Osmotic communication** | Picking up useful information by **overhearing** teammates in a shared space |
| **Caves and commons** | **Commons:** a shared area for working together. **Caves:** quiet places for focused or private work. |
| **Fishbowl window** | A permanent video link between two locations, so remote teammates can see and talk to each other all day |
| **Remote pairing** | Two people working on one item through screen sharing |
| **Information radiator** | A large, visible display of the team's status |

Remote teams cost more in communication, so they need deliberate habits: regular check-ins, inclusive facilitation, shared working hours and good collaboration tools.

## Psychological safety

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.2

**Psychological safety** is a shared belief that the team is a safe place for **interpersonal risk-taking**: speaking up, asking questions, sharing ideas, admitting mistakes and suggesting changes **without fear of embarrassment or retaliation**.

- A large study of more than 180 teams (Google's Project Aristotle) found it to be the **most critical** factor in team effectiveness: **how a team interacts matters more than who is on it**.
- With it, problems are raised **early**. Without it, there is silence, hidden problems and rework.

Four stages, each building on the one before:

| Stage | People feel safe to |
|---|---|
| 1. **Inclusion safety** | **Belong**: be accepted as a member of the team |
| 2. **Learner safety** | **Learn**: ask questions, experiment, make mistakes |
| 3. **Contributor safety** | **Contribute**: offer ideas and take part in decisions |
| 4. **Challenger safety** | **Challenge**: question assumptions and suggest changes |

| Threats to it | What builds it |
|---|---|
| Public criticism and blame | Leaders **admitting their own mistakes** and asking for help |
| Hierarchy: people expect their input to be dismissed | **Team working agreements** written together |
| Deadline pressure that makes people hide concerns | **Blameless retrospectives** |
| Dropping retrospectives | Making space for **quieter voices** |
| Remote work with no informal contact | Regular check-ins; anonymous feedback where needed |

## Levels of conflict

@source Agile Practice Guide, 4.3.1

Some conflict is healthy: a team that can disagree openly makes better decisions. PMI's guide asks the leader to use emotional intelligence to **surface concerns early** and **de-escalate** tense moments. It does not give a scale for conflict. The five-level model below is widely taught in agile courses and comes from outside the guide:

| Level | Name | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Problem to solve** | Sharing information and collaborating; open, fact-based language |
| 2 | **Disagreement** | People protect themselves; language becomes guarded |
| 3 | **Contest** | The aim becomes **winning**; personal attacks begin |
| 4 | **Crusade** | Protecting one's own group; language becomes ideological |
| 5 | **World war** | The aim is to destroy the other side; little or no communication |

The earlier the level, the easier it is to resolve. At levels 1 to 3 the team can usually work it out with support. At levels 4 and 5 outside intervention is needed.

## Exam tips

- "What should the agile leader do?" Look for **remove impediments**, **facilitate**, **coach**, **shield the team**. Avoid answers where the leader **assigns tasks** or **decides for the team**.
- The order is **purpose, people, process**.
- A team member is stuck: the servant leader **helps remove the obstacle** or coaches the team to solve it, and does not take the work over.
- Ideal team size: **three to nine**. Ideal allocation: **100%**.
- "Deep in one skill and able to help in others" = **T-shaped**, a **generalizing specialist**.
- Someone split across two projects is **less than half** as useful to each.
- Team members are afraid to report bad news: the problem is **psychological safety**.
- A team that analyzes everything, then designs everything, then builds everything inside one sprint is doing a **mini-waterfall**.
- **Supportive leader**, **servant leader**, **team facilitator**, **scrum master** and **agile coach** all point to the same kind of role.
