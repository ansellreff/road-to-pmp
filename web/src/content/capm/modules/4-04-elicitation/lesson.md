## Key points

- **Elicitation** means **drawing out** information from stakeholders and other sources. It is more than "gathering": people often cannot state what they need until they are helped to.
- It has **four processes**: determine the elicitation approach, prepare for elicitation, conduct elicitation, confirm elicitation results.
- Elicitation and **analysis alternate**: elicit, analyze, find new questions, elicit again.
- Every elicitation activity has an **introduction, a body and a close**, then a **follow-up**.
- Results are **unconfirmed** until the participants agree they are accurate. Then they are **confirmed**.
- Match the technique to the situation: **interviews** for depth, **workshops** for agreement across groups, **surveys** for large numbers, **observation** for what people cannot describe, **prototypes** for early feedback.
- Use **more than one source and more than one technique**.

## What elicitation is

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Key Concepts for Elicitation; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Table 1-2

**Elicitation** is the activity of **drawing out information** from stakeholders and other sources.

Why not "collecting" or "gathering"? Those words suggest the information is **ready and waiting**. In practice stakeholders have wants, needs and ideas but often **cannot express them clearly**. The analyst and the stakeholders work back and forth until they share an understanding.

| # | Process | Main output | Process group |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Determine Elicitation Approach** | Elicitation approach | Planning |
| 2 | **Prepare for Elicitation** | Elicitation preparation materials | Executing |
| 3 | **Conduct Elicitation** | Unconfirmed elicitation results | Executing |
| 4 | **Confirm Elicitation Results** | Confirmed elicitation results | Executing |

**Elicitation is cyclical.**

- It is repeated for each **level** of information: first business requirements, then stakeholder requirements, then solution requirements.
- It is repeated for each **source**, to get several viewpoints and fill gaps.
- It alternates with **analysis**. Analysis removes what is irrelevant and raises new questions, which need more elicitation.
- The cycle stops when analysis raises **no more questions**, or when the **risk of incomplete information is acceptable**.

## Determine Elicitation Approach

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.1.3.1

The process of thinking through **how** elicitation will be done: which stakeholders, which techniques, and in what order. Benefits: **efficient use of stakeholders' time**, effective collaboration and an organized approach.

The **elicitation approach** answers five questions:

| Question | Example |
|---|---|
| **What** information is needed? | How claims are processed today |
| **Where** is it? | Claims staff, the procedures manual, the current system |
| **How** will it be obtained? | Observation, then a workshop |
| **When**? | Before the design workshop in week 3 |
| **Who** takes part? | Two claims handlers and their supervisor |

- It may be a document (an **elicitation plan** in formal life cycles) or just a thought process, shared with the team.
- The **stakeholder register** helps: a person's role shows what they can tell you, and senior people are harder to schedule.
- **Cutting elicitation short** to save time adds risk of rework and defects. Get early agreement from managers on how much is appropriate.

Techniques used to build the approach: brainstorming, interviews, and retrospectives and lessons learned.

## Prepare for Elicitation

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.2; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.2.3.1

The process of **organizing and scheduling resources and preparing materials** for one elicitation activity. Benefits: the activity runs well, and participants know **why they are there and what is expected**.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| **1. Determine the objective** | Decide why this activity is being held. It must be worth the participants' time. |
| **2. Determine the participants** | The wrong people may mean rescheduling or missing the objective |
| **3. Identify the resources** | Access to systems, documents, a room |
| **4. Identify the questions** | Prepare them, and decide how answers will be recorded |
| **5. Set the agenda** | Send topics and timing in advance, with any pre-work |
| **6. Schedule the activity** | Allow enough time for each stakeholder group |

- **Elicitation preparation materials** include the objective, agenda, questions, ground rules, and presentation material or models.
- For a large workshop, preparation may take **longer than the workshop itself**. For document analysis it is brief.
- Sharing questions beforehand lets stakeholders gather their thoughts.

## Conduct Elicitation

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3; Business Analysis for Practitioners, How to Elicit Solution Information

The process of applying elicitation techniques to draw out information. Its output is the **unconfirmed elicitation results**: notes, sketches, models, photos of whiteboards, recordings.

**Every activity has three stages, then a follow-up:**

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Introduction** | Sets the stage and the purpose; builds rapport |
| **Body** | The questions are asked and answered. Active listening matters most here. |
| **Close** | A graceful end: next steps, action items, thanks |
| **Follow-up** | The notes are consolidated and **confirmed** with the participants |

Three good closing questions: "Do you have any other questions?", "Is there anything we missed?", "Is there anyone else we should talk to?"

**Four kinds of question:**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Open-ended** | Can be answered in any way | "How do you handle a late order?" |
| **Closed-ended** | Answer comes from a limited list | "Do you approve refunds yourself: yes or no?" |
| **Contextual** | About the subject at hand | "Which screen slows you down most?" |
| **Context-free** | Could be asked in any situation | "What would success look like for you?" |

## Elicitation techniques

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3.2.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3.2.6; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3.2.7; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3.2.9

| Technique | What it is | Strength | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | A group lists many ideas in a short time, led by a facilitator | More ideas than people produce alone | Ideas still need sorting |
| **Document analysis** | Studying existing documents for relevant information | **Objective**; saves stakeholder time; a good start before meeting people | Documents may be **out of date** |
| **Facilitated workshop** | A structured meeting with selected stakeholders and a **neutral facilitator**. Also called a requirements workshop. | **Fast** definition across functions; **reconciles differences**; builds consensus | Needs a skilled facilitator and the right people |
| **Focus group** | **Prequalified** stakeholders and experts give their expectations and attitudes about a proposed solution | Direct feedback from customers or users | **Group pressure** from strong voices |
| **Interview** | Prepared or spontaneous questions put to stakeholders, usually one to one | Depth; builds trust; lets people speak candidly | Time-consuming |
| **Observation** | Watching people do their work in their own setting. Also called **job shadowing**. | Finds what people **cannot or will not describe** | People may **act differently** when watched |
| **Prototyping** | A model of the expected solution, shown before it is built | **Early feedback**; makes ideas tangible | Can be mistaken for the finished product |
| **Questionnaires and surveys** | Written questions sent to many respondents | **Many people, wide area, low cost** | **Low response rate** |
| **Collaborative games** | Structured play that encourages participation and creativity | Engagement; indirect way to raise problems | Needs preparation |

**Interviews:**

| Type | How it runs |
|---|---|
| **Structured** | A prepared list of questions, all of which are asked |
| **Unstructured** | Only the first question is fixed; each next question follows from the last answer |

**Prototypes:**

| Kind | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Storyboard** | A series of images showing sequence or navigation |
| **Wireframe** | A static blueprint of a screen layout |
| **Evolutionary** | The real solution in progress, refined until it is finished |

**Collaborative games:**

| Game | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Product box** | Teams design the product's packaging. Shows which features customers value most. |
| **Speedboat** | Problems are written on anchors holding back a boat. A non-confrontational way to find what users dislike. |
| **Spider web** | Draws the relationships between the product and other products |

## Choosing a technique

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3.2.3

| Situation | Best fit |
|---|---|
| Several departments disagree about what is needed | **Facilitated workshop** |
| One expert holds detailed knowledge | **Interview** |
| Thousands of users in many places | **Questionnaire or survey** |
| Staff cannot explain how they do a detailed task | **Observation** |
| Users cannot picture the solution | **Prototype** |
| You need background before meeting anyone | **Document analysis** |
| You want customers' reactions to a proposed product | **Focus group** |
| You need many ideas quickly | **Brainstorming** |

Two rules:

- Use **at least two sources** for each topic, so no requirement rests on one opinion.
- Use **several techniques**: they suit different people and reveal unstated needs.

## Confirm Elicitation Results

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.4.3.1

The process of **following up** on the results: reviewing them with stakeholders for **accuracy and completeness** and comparing them with historical information. Its benefit: it checks that the stakeholders **were understood**.

| | Unconfirmed results | Confirmed results |
|---|---|---|
| **State** | What was captured | What the team **agrees is accurate** |
| **Output of** | Conduct Elicitation | Confirm Elicitation Results |

| Technique | Use in confirming |
|---|---|
| **Walkthrough** | A peer review in which the **author leads reviewers** through the material |
| **Interview** | A follow-up to clear up discrepancies |
| **Document analysis** | Compare the results with existing procedures |
| **Observation** | Check what people said against what they actually do |
| **Glossary** | Agree what terms mean; find terms used differently by different groups |

Confirmation can happen **afterwards**, or **during** the elicitation itself. Results are usually confirmed with subject matter experts, the product owner or the sponsor.

Confirming is **not approving**. It only establishes that the notes are an accurate record. Requirements are approved later.

## Predictive and adaptive elicitation

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.3.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 6.1.4

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | **Mostly up front**, in an analysis phase | In **every iteration**, in short cycles |
| **How much** | Comprehensive | **Just enough** |
| **Looks ahead** | The whole product | The current iteration and **one to two iterations ahead** |
| **Approach** | A high-level approach early, written into the business analysis plan | Decided early in each iteration; not a separate deliverable |
| **Results** | Documented | May not be documented, or documented just enough |
| **Confirmation** | Often at a later point | Usually as elicitation happens |

## Exam tips

- "Drawing out", not "gathering". Stakeholders often do not know what they need.
- Order: **approach, prepare, conduct, confirm**.
- Results from a workshop are **unconfirmed** until reviewed with participants.
- A **walkthrough** is led by the **author** of the material.
- People cannot describe their own work accurately: use **observation**.
- Conflicting needs across departments: use a **facilitated workshop**.
- A large, dispersed group: use a **survey**. Its weakness is the **response rate**.
- Early feedback before building: use a **prototype**.
- The main weakness of observation: **people behave differently when watched**.
- The main weakness of a focus group: **group pressure**.
- A workshop facilitator should be **neutral**.
- A document contradicts what a stakeholder said: **check with a second source**. Documents are often out of date.
