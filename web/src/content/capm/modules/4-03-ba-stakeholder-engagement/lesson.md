## Key points

- **Stakeholder engagement** in business analysis means working out **who must be involved**, **understanding them**, and **keeping them involved** so the right solution is built.
- It has **seven processes**: identify stakeholders, conduct stakeholder analysis, determine the engagement and communication approach, conduct business analysis planning, prepare for transition to the future state, manage engagement and communication, assess business analysis performance.
- The **stakeholder register** lists the stakeholders and what is known about them. **A missed stakeholder means missed requirements.**
- A **persona** is a **fictional** character standing for a class of users. A stakeholder is a **real** person.
- **RACI**: Responsible, Accountable, Consult, Inform. Only **one** role is accountable for a task.
- The **business analysis plan** covers the whole approach. It is **broader** than a requirements management plan.
- **Transition** needs a **readiness assessment** and a **transition plan**.
- The project manager analyzes stakeholders for impact on the **project**; the business analyst for impact on the **solution**. They should do it **together**.

## What stakeholder engagement is

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 1.3.3; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Table 1-2

Stakeholder engagement covers the activities that analyze the needs and characteristics of stakeholders, to understand how best to **identify, involve, communicate with and work with them**. Its aim is the right representation and **continuing interest and involvement** from the stakeholder community.

| # | Process | Main output | Process group |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Identify Stakeholders** | Stakeholder register | Defining and Aligning |
| 2 | **Conduct Stakeholder Analysis** | Updated stakeholder register | Planning |
| 3 | **Determine Stakeholder Engagement and Communication Approach** | The approach | Planning |
| 4 | **Conduct Business Analysis Planning** | Business analysis plan | Planning |
| 5 | **Prepare for Transition to Future State** | Readiness assessment; transition plan | Executing |
| 6 | **Manage Stakeholder Engagement and Communication** | Improved engagement and communication | Monitoring and Controlling |
| 7 | **Assess Business Analysis Performance** | Performance assessment | Monitoring and Controlling |

This knowledge area **runs through all the others**. Much of business analysis is communication.

## Identify Stakeholders

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.1.3.1

The process of identifying the individuals, groups or organizations that **may impact, are impacted, or are perceived to be impacted** by the area under assessment. It decides **whose interests must be taken into account**.

- The output is the **stakeholder register**: anyone who may affect, be affected by, or think they are affected by the **proposed solution**.
- It starts early, when the business need is being defined, and the list is **revisited** whenever scope changes or analysis turns up someone new.

| Technique | How it helps find stakeholders |
|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | A group lists as many as it can in a short time |
| **Interviews** | Known stakeholders name others |
| **Organizational charts** | Show the departments and roles that may be affected |
| **Process flows** | Show who performs a process or uses its outputs, now and in the future state |
| **Questionnaires and surveys** | Reach many people quickly to build or check the list |

Enterprise and business architects can supply models of organizational units, roles and skills.

## Conduct Stakeholder Analysis

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.3.1

The process of researching and analyzing **quantitative and qualitative information** about the stakeholders. Its benefit: insight used to **choose elicitation and analysis techniques**, decide **whom to involve and when**, and pick the best **communication and collaboration methods**.

A stakeholder register may record, for each stakeholder:

| Characteristic | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Role** | What they can tell you and decide |
| **Attitude** | Supportive, neutral or resistant to the solution |
| **Interests** | What they want from it |
| **Level of impact** | How much the solution will change their work |
| **Level of influence** | How much they can shape the requirements |
| **Communication preferences** | How and how often to reach them |
| **Success criteria** | What "good" looks like to them |
| **Work hours and location** | When and where they are available |

**Engagement levels** describe where a stakeholder stands, now and where you need them to be: **unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading**.

**Job analysis** identifies the requirements and competencies of a job or role. It is useful when the solution changes how people work or creates a new role.

## Personas

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.2.2

A **persona** is a **fictional character** created to represent an individual or a group of stakeholders, called a **user class**.

| | Stakeholder | Persona |
|---|---|---|
| **Real or fictional** | A real person | A fictional representation |
| **Detail** | Role, interest, influence | Much more about **how they work**: goals, behaviors, skills, device literacy, how often they do a task |
| **Interest in the outcome** | Has one | May have none; can be a user with no influence |

- It can include a name, a narrative, goals, behaviors, motivations, environment and skills.
- It may be a paragraph or one to two pages.
- Used when it is **not possible to speak to every user**: group them into classes and build a persona for each.
- Build personas only for the **most critical or most affected** groups.

Example: "Rina, 34, a night-shift nurse. Enters patient notes on a shared tablet, about forty times a shift, usually standing. Has no time for training during work."

## The RACI model

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.2.3

A **RACI model** is a responsibility assignment matrix showing each stakeholder's involvement in each activity.

| Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **R, Responsible** | **Performs** the task |
| **A, Accountable** | Answers for the completion and quality of the task; the **final approver** |
| **C, Consult** | **Gives information** needed to complete the task |
| **I, Inform** | Affected by the task, so is **kept up to date** |

| Activity | Analyst | Sponsor | Product owner | SMEs | Project manager |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elicit requirements | **R** | I | **A** | C | I |
| Approve requirements | C | **A** | **R** | C | I |

- It reduces confusion and conflict where responsibilities seem to overlap.
- It shows **single points of accountability**.
- A stakeholder's involvement can differ from one activity to another.

## Stakeholder maps

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.2.2.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, Figure 5-6

Stakeholder maps show how stakeholders relate **to each other and to the solution**.

**Stakeholder matrix.** A two-by-two grid. The team chooses the two variables. One common pair is **influence** (how much the stakeholder can shape the requirements) and **impact** (how much the solution will affect them).

| | **Low impact** | **High impact** |
|---|---|---|
| **High influence** | Possible **champions** and advocates: decision makers not directly affected. Keep communication open and use their support. | The **critical** group and a key source of requirements. Give them the **most effort**, communicate often, build a partnership. |
| **Low influence** | Do not ignore. **Monitor** in case their position changes. Their requirements may rank low. | Also a **critical source of requirements**: often the people who must live with the solution. Make sure communication reaches them and their concerns are heard. |

**Onion diagram.** Rings around the solution show how close each stakeholder is to it.

| Layer | Who |
|---|---|
| **Center** | The solution |
| **Layer 1** | Those directly involved in developing it |
| **Layer 2** | Those whose work is directly changed by it |
| **Layer 3** | Those who work with the people directly affected |
| **Outer** | External stakeholders: customers, regulators, suppliers |

The closer to the center, the stronger the relationship to the solution.

## Determine the engagement and communication approach

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.3; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.3.3.1

The process of working out how to **engage and communicate with stakeholders throughout the product life cycle**, based on the analysis of their needs, interests and roles. The benefit: stakeholders get **the right information, by the best method, at the right frequency**.

The approach records what was agreed on:

- the **level of involvement** of each stakeholder or group
- how **decisions are made** and **approvals obtained**
- how product and project information is **structured, stored and maintained**
- how stakeholders are **kept up to date**

**Choosing a channel.** Match the method to the audience and the purpose:

| Need | Suitable channel |
|---|---|
| Work out complex or disputed requirements | **Facilitated workshop**, face to face where possible |
| A decision from senior managers | A short **presentation** with a clear recommendation |
| Regular progress for many people | A **report** or dashboard |
| Detailed input from one expert | An **interview** |
| Views from a large or dispersed group | A **survey** |
| A team working in one place | **Conversation**, with visible boards |

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **Form** | Written, as part of the business analysis plan, before elicitation starts | Agreed **verbally** by the team; the **product owner** represents stakeholder needs |
| **Communication** | Formats and detail planned per stakeholder group | Mostly in person when the team is colocated |

Senior managers know best where stakeholder priorities conflict, so the approach is checked with them.

## Conduct Business Analysis Planning

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.4; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.4.3.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.4.4

The process of reaching **shared agreement** on the business analysis activities to be performed and on roles, responsibilities and skills. It **sets expectations**.

It does three things:

1. **Brings together** the approaches from every knowledge area into one set of decisions
2. **Estimates** the effort for business analysis activities
3. **Assembles** the business analysis plan

| | Business analysis plan | Requirements management plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Covers** | The **whole** business analysis approach, from stakeholder engagement to managing requirements | Only how requirements are elicited, analyzed, documented and managed |
| **Scope** | Broader | Narrower |

- It may be a subplan of the project management plan or a separate plan, formal or informal.
- **Explain why** each planning choice was made.
- Build it **with** key stakeholders and the project team, to gain buy-in.
- Planning overlaps with the project manager's work (stakeholders, communication, risk, estimating). **Work together** to avoid doing it twice or inconsistently.

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **Estimating** | A separate estimate for each business analysis task | Analysis effort is **not estimated separately**; it is part of each backlog item |
| **Planning basis** | Tasks scheduled in a **work breakdown structure** | **Prioritization** at release level; just-in-time estimating each iteration |
| **Deliverable** | A business analysis plan, approved before elicitation | **Rarely a separate deliverable** |

**Rolling wave planning** plans near-term work in detail and later work at a higher level. It is used in both approaches.

## Prepare for Transition to Future State

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.5; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.5.3.1; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.5.3.2

The process of determining **whether the organization is ready** for a transition and **how it will move** from the current state to the future state. Its benefit: the organization can **successfully adopt** the changes.

| Output | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Readiness assessment** | Determines the organization's **ability and interest** to move to the future state. Finds **gaps in readiness**, which are treated as risks. May be a report or a checklist. |
| **Transition plan** | Based on the readiness assessment and the transition strategy. Holds actionable, testable **transition requirements**. |

**Transition strategies:**

| Strategy | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Massive one-time cutover** | Everyone moves to the new solution at once |
| **Staged release** | Rolled out by segment: region, customer type or user type |
| **Time-boxed coexistence** | Old and new run together until a final cutover date |
| **Permanent coexistence** | Old and new continue side by side |

The transition plan covers **communication, rollout, training, procedure updates and business recovery updates**. It often includes a checklist of activities with latest completion dates. The rollout must come at a time the business can absorb it and must not clash with other releases.

A successful release needs two things: the **solution working as expected**, and the **people ready to use it**.

In adaptive work, transition may be tasks inside a story, separate transition stories, or part of the **definition of done**. A large-scale release still needs formal planning.

## Manage Stakeholder Engagement and Communication

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.6; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.6.3.1

The process of **fostering appropriate involvement**, keeping stakeholders informed about the business analysis work, and **sharing product information as it evolves**.

- It **monitors participation**: are stakeholders still engaged, and involved enough to understand the requirements?
- When engagement drops, the analyst works with **those who can remove roadblocks**: the project manager, the sponsor, functional managers.
- The **sponsor** has the authority to make sure stakeholders keep fulfilling their role.

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **How concerns surface** | Raised with stakeholders directly, or at status and risk meetings | In the short **daily team meeting** (15 minutes at most); roadblocks are removed straight away |

## Assess Business Analysis Performance

@source PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.7; PMI Guide to Business Analysis, 5.7.3.1

The process of considering **how effective the business analysis practices are**. Its benefit is the chance to **adjust the practices** to fit the project, the team and the organization.

- Practices that work well can become **standards** for the whole organization.
- Techniques that cause trouble are improved or **replaced**.
- Concerns about people are handled with **training or coaching**.

Techniques: **retrospectives and lessons learned**, **root cause analysis**, **variance analysis** (planned against actual), burndown charts and process flows.

| | Predictive | Adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | At milestones or the end of the work, as lessons learned. Findings mostly help **future** projects. | At least at the **end of every iteration** ("inspect and adapt"). Changes apply to the **next iteration**. |

## Exam tips

- A new stakeholder group is discovered mid-project: **update the stakeholder register and analyze them**, then elicit their requirements.
- Fictional and detailed is a **persona**. Real is a **stakeholder**.
- **Accountable** is the final approver. **Responsible** does the work.
- High influence and high impact: **the most effort and the most frequent communication**.
- Low influence but high impact: **still a critical source of requirements**. Do not neglect them.
- Stakeholders closest to the center of an **onion diagram** have the strongest relationship to the solution.
- A stakeholder stops attending workshops: this is **Manage Stakeholder Engagement and Communication**. Find out why, and involve those who can remove the roadblock.
- "Is the organization ready for the change?" is answered by the **readiness assessment**.
- Training and data conversion needs go into the **transition plan** as **transition requirements**.
- The business analysis plan is **broader** than the requirements management plan.
- The analyst and the project manager both analyze stakeholders: they should **do it together**, so stakeholders are not asked twice.
