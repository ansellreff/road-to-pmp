## Key points

- **Organizational governance** directs and controls the whole organization. **Project governance** is the adaptable framework that guides one project inside it.
- **Enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)** are conditions the project team does *not* control that influence, constrain or direct the project. They can be internal or external to the organization.
- **Organizational process assets (OPAs)** are the organization's own plans, processes, templates and knowledge. They are always internal, and the team can use and often update them.
- OPAs come in two groups: **policies, processes and procedures** (set outside the project) and **organizational knowledge repositories** (updated throughout the project).
- The **organizational structure** decides how much authority the project manager has: lowest in functional structures, highest in project-oriented ones.
- A **PMO** can be **supportive** (low control), **controlling** (moderate) or **directive** (high: it manages the projects itself).

## Two levels of governance

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 1.3.2

**Organizational governance** provides direction and control through policies, processes, procedures and decisions, so the organization meets its strategic and operational goals. It is usually overseen by an executive committee or senior leaders, and it is shaped by laws, industry regulations, standards and the organization's own policies.

It affects portfolios, programs and projects by:

- enforcing legal, regulatory and compliance requirements
- defining ethical, social and environmental responsibilities
- setting operational, legal, financial and risk policies
- keeping portfolios, programs and projects aligned with strategic objectives
- making sure initiatives contribute to the mission and vision
- supporting decisions that maximize delivered value

**Project governance** is the adaptable framework that guides project management activities so the project creates value in line with those organizational goals. It supplies:

- structure, systems and processes
- roles and responsibilities
- decision-making models, such as a **RACI matrix** (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) or a **governance board**
- accountability and communication rules

Project governance also helps prioritize initiatives and allocate resources. A typical example is a **steering committee** on a product launch that oversees milestones, enforces quality standards and resolves escalated issues.

| | Organizational governance | Project governance |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | The entire organization | One project |
| Overseen by | Executive committee, senior leaders | Sponsor, steering committee, governance board |
| Purpose | Direction and control for strategy and operations | Framework to manage the project and create value |
| Relationship | Sets the rules | Works within those rules |

## The project environment

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2; PMBOK 7, Standard 2.4; Process Groups: A Practice Guide, 2.1; Process Groups: A Practice Guide, 2.2

Every project operates inside an internal and an external environment. These influences can be favorable, unfavorable or neutral. PMI sorts them into two categories: **EEFs** and **OPAs**.

> **On the exam.** The two exam books describe the environment differently. *Process Groups: A Practice Guide* uses **EEFs** and **OPAs**, as this lesson does. The **7th edition** does not use those two terms: it speaks of the **internal environment** and the **external environment**. Its internal list puts process assets, governance documentation, data assets, knowledge assets, and security and safety measures side by side with culture, infrastructure, IT software, resource availability and employee capability. Its external list adds **commercial databases** (standard cost-estimating data, industry risk studies). A question may use either vocabulary.

## Enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2.1

EEFs are **conditions not directly influenced by the project team that impact, constrain or direct the project**. They are inputs to many project management processes, especially planning. They can help or limit your options, and they influence how you tailor your approach.

**Internal EEFs** (inside the organization):

- organizational culture, structure and governance: vision, values, leadership style, hierarchy, code of conduct
- geographic distribution of facilities and resources, including virtual or hybrid teams
- infrastructure: facilities, equipment, IT hardware
- information technology systems: scheduling tools, configuration management, work authorization systems
- resource availability: staffing levels, purchasing constraints
- employee capability: skills and expertise
- financial capability of the organization

**External EEFs** (outside the organization):

- marketplace conditions: competitors, customer behavior, market share
- social and cultural influences: political climate, ethics, perceptions
- legal restrictions: laws on data protection, employment, procurement
- academic research: industry studies, benchmarking results
- government or industry standards
- financial considerations: exchange rates, interest rates, inflation, taxes
- physical environmental elements: weather, working conditions
- emerging technologies: AI, automation
- public health and safety regulations

> The test for an EEF: can the project team change it? If not, and it still affects the project, it is an EEF. The team must work with it or around it.

## Organizational process assets (OPAs)

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2.2; PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2.3; PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2.4

OPAs are the **plans, processes, documents, templates and knowledge repositories** specific to and used by the performing organization. They include lessons learned and historical information from earlier projects. They are inputs to many processes.

OPAs are **internal** to the organization, so the team can often update or add to them. They fall into two groups:

**1. Policies, processes and procedures.** These are usually established by the PMO or another function outside the project and are generally *not* updated as part of the project work. They change only by following organizational policy. The team may be encouraged to **tailor** them. Examples:

- tailoring guidelines
- project life cycles, methods, checklists
- templates (project management plan, risk register, stakeholder register, contracts)
- preapproved supplier lists
- change control procedures
- issue and defect management processes
- project closure guidelines

**2. Organizational knowledge repositories.** These *are* updated throughout the project with project information. Examples:

- lessons learned and historical information
- financial data (labor hours, costs, budgets, overruns)
- issue and defect data
- configuration management records (versions and baselines)
- measurement data and metrics
- project files from previous projects (schedules, baselines, risk registers)

## EEF or OPA?

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2

| | EEF | OPA |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A condition around the project | An asset the organization owns |
| Where it comes from | Inside or outside the organization | Inside the organization only |
| Can the team change it? | No | Often yes (knowledge repositories), or by following policy |
| Effect | Constrains or directs | Helps and guides |
| Examples | Culture, laws, market conditions, existing IT systems | Templates, procedures, lessons learned, historical data |

A quick way to decide: a **law, a market, a culture or an existing system** is an EEF. A **template, a procedure or a lesson learned** is an OPA.

## Organizational structures

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2.5; PMBOK 8, Standard 2.2.5, Table 2-1; Process Groups: A Practice Guide, 2.5.1; Process Groups: A Practice Guide, 2.5.2

There is no one-size-fits-all structure. The right one depends on the organization's size, industry, geographic spread, strategy and project complexity. What matters for the exam is how each structure affects the project manager.

| Structure | Work is grouped by | PM's authority | PM's role | Resource availability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic or simple | People working side by side | Low | Part-time | Low |
| Functional (centralized) | Job being done (engineering, finance) | Low | Part-time | Low |
| Multidivisional | Product, region, customer type | Low | Part-time | Low |
| Matrix, weak | Job function | Low | Part-time, done as part of another job | Low |
| Matrix, balanced | Job function | Low to moderate | Part-time, embedded in the function | Low to moderate |
| Matrix, strong | Job function, with project manager as a function | Moderate to high | Full-time | Moderate to high |
| Project-oriented | Project | High to almost total | Full-time | High to almost total |
| Virtual / network | Network with points of contact | Low to moderate | Full-time or part-time | Low to moderate |
| Hybrid | Mix of other types | Mixed | Mixed | Mixed |

How to remember it:

- In a **functional** structure, people report to a functional manager (head of engineering, head of finance). The project manager has little authority and often acts as a coordinator.
- In a **matrix** structure, team members have two bosses: the functional manager and the project manager. Weak, balanced and strong describe how much power the project manager has.
- In a **project-oriented** structure, the team works for the project manager, who has near-total authority over people and budget.

> **On the exam.** *Process Groups: A Practice Guide* has the same table. The outline asks which structure **suits a predictive, plan-based project**. A **project-oriented** or **strong matrix** structure suits it best: the project manager has the authority, the budget and the dedicated people needed to carry out a detailed plan. In a **functional** or **weak matrix** structure the functional manager controls the people and the budget, so the project manager coordinates and must negotiate for resources. The guide lists **physical location** (colocated, regional, virtual) among the factors in choosing a structure: a **virtual** team needs more deliberate communication planning than a **colocated** one.

## The project management office (PMO)

@source Process Groups: A Practice Guide, 2.6

A **PMO** is an organizational structure that **standardizes project-related governance processes** and makes it easier to **share resources, methods, tools and techniques**. What it does ranges from giving support to managing projects directly.

| Type | What it does | Control |
|---|---|---|
| **Supportive** | **Consults**: supplies templates, best practices, training, access to information and lessons learned. Acts as a project repository | **Low** |
| **Controlling** | Gives support and **requires compliance**: adopting a framework or method, using set templates, forms and tools, conforming to governance | **Moderate** |
| **Directive** | **Manages the projects itself**. Project managers are assigned by the PMO and report to it | **High** |

A PMO supports project managers by managing shared resources across projects, developing the method and standards, coaching and training, auditing compliance, keeping the shared templates and procedures (organizational process assets), and coordinating communication between projects.

A PMO may also have the authority to act as a key decision maker through the life of a project, to keep it aligned with business objectives. It may make recommendations, lead knowledge transfer, and **terminate projects**. The projects under one PMO need not be related to each other.

## Exam tips

- PMO types by control: **supportive = low** (advises), **controlling = moderate** (requires compliance), **directive = high** (runs the projects).
- "Lessons learned", "templates", "historical information" and "procedures" are OPAs. "Organizational culture", "market conditions", "government regulations" and "existing infrastructure" are EEFs.
- Organizational culture is *internal* but still an EEF, because the team cannot change it. Internal does not automatically mean OPA.
- If a question describes a project manager with little authority who must negotiate with department heads for people, think functional or weak matrix.
- Project governance works *inside* organizational governance. A project cannot override organizational policy.
