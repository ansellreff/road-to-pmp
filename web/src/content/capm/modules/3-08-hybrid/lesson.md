## Key points

- A **hybrid approach** combines elements of **predictive** and **adaptive** (agile) approaches in one project.
- Approaches sit on a **continuum**. Hybrid is any point between fully predictive and fully agile.
- The approach is chosen to **fit the work**: predictive where work is well understood, agile where **requirements or technology are uncertain**.
- There are **four hybrid patterns**: agile then predictive; both at the same time; mostly predictive with an agile part; mostly agile with a predictive part.
- The **Stacey matrix** and the **Cynefin framework** help judge how complex the work is.
- The **agile suitability filter** scores **culture, team and project** on a radar chart. It is a **conversation tool**, not a verdict.
- Teams also **blend agile approaches**, most commonly Scrum, Kanban and XP practices together.

## What a hybrid approach is

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.3; Agile Practice Guide, Glossary (hybrid approach)

A **hybrid approach** is a combination of elements from both adaptive and predictive approaches. It is useful when there is **uncertainty or risk around the requirements**, when deliverables can be **split into modules**, or when different deliverables are built by **different teams**.

| Approach | Requirements | Delivery | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predictive** | Defined up front | Once, at the end | Controlled |
| **Hybrid** | Partly fixed, partly evolving | Mixed | Controlled in some parts, welcomed in others |
| **Adaptive (agile)** | Evolve throughout | Frequent, small increments | Expected |

The PMBOK Guide's position is that **most projects today benefit from a hybrid approach**. Using only one approach rarely covers everything a modern project needs: hybrid gives **flexibility** where conditions change and **control** where the work is predictable.

A project that uses short iterations, daily coordination meetings and retrospectives, but still estimates up front and assigns work centrally, is **hybrid**. Calling it agile would be misleading, because it does not fully reflect the agile mindset. Calling it predictive would be inaccurate too.

## Matching the approach to the work

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.10

| Kind of work | Example | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Defined and repeatable**, little uncertainty | Building a standard house | **Predictive**: plan the work, then work the plan |
| **Novel and intangible** (knowledge work) | Software, a new service, a film | **Adaptive**: discover the solution through feedback |

Trouble starts when a predictive approach is used for new, unfamiliar work. People cannot point to a physical reference ("a door like this one, but taller"), so understanding differs. The result is more **change requests, defects, uncertainty and risk**.

## Judging complexity: Stacey and Cynefin

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.1

**The Stacey matrix** plots two kinds of uncertainty:

- **Requirements uncertainty:** how much agreement there is on **what** is needed
- **Technical uncertainty:** how certain the team is about **how** to deliver it

| Zone | Requirements and technology | Approach that fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | Both certain | **Predictive** |
| **Complicated** | One or both less certain | Adaptive, or hybrid |
| **Complex** | Both uncertain | **Adaptive** |
| **Chaotic** | Both highly uncertain | Fundamentally risky; no approach is safe |

**The Cynefin framework** sorts situations into five domains:

| Domain | Cause and effect | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | Obvious | Apply good practice |
| **Complicated** | Found by expert analysis | Analyze, then act. A hybrid life cycle may fit. |
| **Complex** | Only emerges through experiments | **Probe, sense, respond**. This matches agile. |
| **Chaotic** | No clear relationship | Act immediately to stabilize |
| **Disorder** | Unclear which domain applies | Work out which domain you are in |

Both tools make the same point: choosing a life cycle is **not a matter of preference**. It is a response to the nature of the problem.

## The four hybrid patterns

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.3; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.4; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.5; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.6; PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.3

| Pattern | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Agile development, then predictive rollout** | Development is uncertain; rollout is defined and repeatable | Develop a new high-tech product, then roll it out and train thousands of users |
| **2. Agile and predictive at the same time** | The team uses some agile practices while other parts stay predictive. Common during a move toward agile. | Short iterations and retrospectives, with up-front estimating and predictive progress tracking |
| **3. Mostly predictive with an agile component** | Most of the work is routine; **one part is uncertain** | A building project that uses a **new roofing material**: trial installations on the ground first |
| **4. Mostly agile with a predictive component** | One element is **non-negotiable** or cannot be done incrementally | Integrating a component from a vendor that delivers once and will not work incrementally |

The PMBOK Guide adds two more examples of hybrid work:

- **A smart building:** the construction is predictive; the building's IT systems are developed adaptively.
- **A hospital records system:** the infrastructure set-up is predictive; the user interface is developed adaptively.

## Three levels of hybrid

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.2.3

PMI's Disciplined Agile tool kit describes three levels:

| Level | Dominant approach | Role of the other approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Hybrid level 1** | **Predictive** | Some adaptive elements, used to ease specific pain points |
| **Hybrid level 2** | **Neither**: both contribute significantly | |
| **Hybrid level 3** | **Adaptive** | Some predictive elements, used to satisfy business constraints |

## Fit for purpose, and hybrid as a transition

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.7; Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.8

**Fit for purpose.** The goal is to produce **business value in the best way for the current environment**. Whether that way is agile or predictive does not matter. Questions to ask:

- How can we be most successful?
- How can we deliver value in short cycles?
- Is **feedback** needed as the team produces value? If so, **increments** help.
- Must **risk** be managed while ideas are explored? If so, **iterations** help.

If the organization **cannot deliver intermediate value**, agile approaches may not be useful. The choice should be **revisited periodically** as context, risk and delivery needs change.

**Hybrid as a transition strategy.** Few organizations can switch to agile overnight. A gradual move works better:

1. Add **iterative** techniques first, to improve learning and alignment.
2. Add **incremental** techniques next, to deliver value sooner.
3. Start on a **less risky project** with low to medium uncertainty.
4. When that works, move to more complex projects.

## Factors in choosing an approach

@source PMBOK 8, Standard 4.3

| Category | Points toward predictive | Points toward adaptive |
|---|---|---|
| **Deliverable** | Requirements well known; change is costly; strict **safety** or **regulatory** needs | High **innovation**; unstable scope; value from frequent **feedback**; can be delivered in parts |
| **Project** | A fixed end date that must be met | Stakeholders available throughout; value in early delivery; **uncertain funding** |
| **Organization** | Hierarchical structure; a culture of managing and directing | Flexible structure; a culture that accepts uncertainty and self-managed teams; small teams |

Because these factors differ across the parts of one project, **several approaches are often used in the same project** for different deliverables. That is the usual origin of a hybrid.

## The agile suitability filter

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.10.2; Agile Practice Guide, X3.1; Agile Practice Guide, X3.2.1; Agile Practice Guide, X3.2.3

The **agile suitability filter** is a questionnaire that helps a group decide whether a project suits a predictive, hybrid or agile approach. Questions fall into three categories:

| Category | What is assessed |
|---|---|
| **Culture** | Buy-in for the approach, trust in the team, how decisions are made |
| **Team** | Team size, experience, access to the customer or business |
| **Project** | Likelihood of change, how critical the product is, whether incremental delivery is possible |

Each question is scored from **1 to 10** and the scores are plotted on a **radar chart**:

| Where the scores fall | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Clustered near the **center** | **Agile** |
| In the **middle** ring | **Hybrid** |
| Around the **outside** | **Predictive** |

How to use it:

- Complete it **as a group**: sponsor, team, customer and governance representatives. A mix of people avoids one person's bias.
- Its value is the **conversation** it starts. It is a high-level diagnostic only.
- If stakeholders agree on a different approach from the one the tool suggests, **follow the stakeholder consensus**.
- Mismatches it reveals are **inputs to risk management**.
- It can be **repeated later** to check that the approach still fits.

## Blending agile approaches

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.11

Agile teams rarely use one framework alone. A framework is a starting point; the team **tailors** it to deliver value regularly.

The most common blend:

| From | The team takes |
|---|---|
| **Scrum** | The product backlog, the roles, and the events: sprint planning, daily scrum, sprint review, sprint retrospective |
| **Kanban** | A board that makes the flow and impediments visible, and **WIP limits** to manage the flow |
| **XP** | Engineering practices: story cards, continuous integration, refactoring, automated testing, test-driven development |

Together they perform better than any one of them alone.

Do not confuse the two kinds of mixing: a **hybrid** combines **predictive and adaptive** approaches; a **blend** combines **several agile** approaches.

## Tailoring to fix a problem

@source Agile Practice Guide, 3.12

| Situation | Tailoring option |
|---|---|
| Stakeholders are **not consistently available** | More frequent, lightweight check-ins; record key decisions openly |
| Requirements are **highly ambiguous** | Short discovery cycles: prototypes, user testing, ongoing backlog refinement |
| Demand is **steady or sporadic** | Use a cadence for demonstrations and retrospectives; flow-based agile with a cadence gives both |
| The team needs to **improve faster** | Hold retrospectives more often |
| Work is **often interrupted or delayed** | Make work visible on a kanban board and try WIP limits |
| **Quality** of increments is poor | Use test-driven development practices |
| **More than one team** is needed | Learn about scaling frameworks, then shape an approach that fits |
| The team is **new to agile** | Train in the agile mindset and principles first, then in the chosen framework |

Tailoring is an experiment: if things improve, keep the change; if they get worse, stop and try something else.

## Troubleshooting an agile project

@source Agile Practice Guide, First Edition, Table 5-1

The 2017 guide lists common pain points and what to try. Exam questions describe the symptom and ask for the remedy.

| Pain point | What to try |
|---|---|
| Unclear purpose or mission | **Agile chartering**: vision, mission and mission tests |
| Unclear working agreements | Chartering for alignment: values, principles and **working agreements** |
| Unclear requirements | Help the sponsor craft a **product vision**; build a product roadmap with user story mapping; decompose it into a backlog of smaller, concrete requirements |
| Inaccurate estimation | **Split stories** to make them smaller; estimate relatively, with the **whole team**; spike what is not understood |
| Unclear work assignments or progress | Help the team learn that it **self-manages**; use a kanban board and a daily standup to see where the work is |
| The team struggles with obstacles | A **servant leader** clears them; obstacles nobody on the team can remove are **escalated** |
| Delays caused by poorly refined backlog items | The product owner and the team workshop stories together; write a **definition of ready** |
| Defects | Pair work, collective ownership, pervasive (test-driven, automated) testing and a robust **definition of done** |
| Work is not complete | Define the **definition of done** for stories, including acceptance criteria; add release criteria for the project |
| Technical debt | **Refactoring**, pervasive testing, automated code quality analysis, the definition of done |
| Slow improvement in teamwork | Capture **no more than three** improvement items at each retrospective |
| Too much upfront work leading to rework | Use team **spikes** to learn instead; shorten iterations |
| Inefficiently ordered backlog | Rank by **value**, including the cost of delay divided by duration (CD3) |
| Rush then wait, an uneven flow of work | Plan to the team's **capacity** and no more; stop multitasking; pair, swarm or mob |
| Impossible stakeholder demands | The servant leader works with the stakeholder, and possibly the product owner |
| Siloed teams | Ask people to self-organize as **cross-functional** teams; help managers see why agile needs them |

## Exam tips

- "Combination of predictive and adaptive" is the definition of **hybrid**.
- Uncertain development followed by a routine rollout: **agile first, then predictive**.
- A mostly routine project with **one new, uncertain part**: predictive with an **agile component**.
- **Strict safety or regulatory** requirements point toward **predictive**. **Unclear requirements** point toward **adaptive**.
- Stacey: both requirements and technology certain means **predictive**.
- Cynefin **complex** domain: **probe, sense, respond**.
- Suitability filter: scores near the **center** suggest agile; near the **outside**, predictive.
- The filter's result is **not binding**. The people involved make the final decision.
- A team moving to agile should start with a **low-risk project** and add techniques gradually.
- The best approach is the one that **fits the project, its risks and the culture**. No approach is best for every project.
